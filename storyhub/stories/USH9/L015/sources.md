# USH9 L015 — Who Showed Up — public source notes

This StoryHub follows four major labor conflicts from 1877 through 1894 and compares the institutions used to stop them. The student page distinguishes quoted primary-source language from later historical interpretation and uses conservative wording where published casualty totals disagree.

## Primary sources

- **Rutherford B. Hayes, diary, August 5, 1877.** Published in *Diary and Letters of Rutherford Birchard Hayes, Nineteenth President of the United States*, Vol. III, edited by Charles R. Williams, Ohio State Archaeological and Historical Society, 1924. Used for Hayes's reflection that the strikes had been put down by force and for his statement about a worker's right to refuse work versus preventing others from working.
- **Statement of the Pullman strikers to the American Railway Union convention, June 1894.** Printed in the United States Strike Commission's *Report on the Chicago Strike of June and July 1894*. Used for the workers' description of their wage/rent situation and their statement that they struck because they were without hope.
- **August Spies, scaffold statement, November 11, 1887.** The line used in the lesson is also inscribed on the Haymarket Martyrs' Monument in Forest Park, Illinois.
- ***In re Debs*, 158 U.S. 564 (1895).** Supreme Court decision of May 27, 1895 upholding federal authority used in the Pullman dispute.
- **Federal injunction of July 2, 1894.** United States Circuit Court for the Northern District of Illinois; Judges Peter S. Grosscup and William A. Woods. Used for the scope of the order against American Railway Union officers.
- **Andrew Carnegie, “An Employer's View of the Labor Question,” *The Forum* (1886).** Used for the documented contrast between Carnegie's published defense of workers' right to combine and the later Homestead lockout under Frick.
- **Governor John Peter Altgeld, pardon message, June 26, 1893.** Used for the later official criticism of the Haymarket trial and the pardons of Fielden, Neebe, and Schwab.

## Institutional and scholarly references

- **United States Department of Labor — history of Labor Day.** Supports the June 28, 1894 federal Labor Day signing date. The lesson does **not** claim the holiday was created to appease Pullman strikers; the bill predated the boycott.
- **West Virginia Encyclopedia — Railroad Strike of 1877.** Supports the Martinsburg sequence: local militia, Governor Mathews's arrival, and federal troops reaching the city on July 19.
- **Library of Congress — This Month in Business History, Homestead Strike.** Used for the Homestead chronology and broad context.
- **Federal Judicial Center — *In re Debs* teaching materials.** Used for the injunction/contempt framework and federal-court chronology.
- **Illinois Labor History Society — Haymarket Affair.** Used for event chronology and biographical context.
- **National Park Service — Pullman National Historical Park.** Used for the company-town structure and to avoid the persistent but incorrect claim that Pullman paid workers in company scrip.
- **Battle of Homestead Foundation — Homestead Works timeline.** Used for the lockout, Pinkerton landing, state-militia occupation, and strike chronology.
- **Paul Avrich, *The Haymarket Tragedy* (Princeton University Press, 1984).** The lesson explicitly attributes to Avrich the conclusion, drawn from medical and trial evidence, that most police officers who died after the Haymarket bombing were struck by police gunfire.

## Deliberate treatment of disputed numbers

- **Great Railroad Strike start:** the page anchors the opening on the B&O wage cut taking effect July 16, 1877. Accounts differ on the precise first walkout and whether Martinsburg or Baltimore came first.
- **1877 national deaths:** published estimates range from roughly 100 to 160. Student-facing copy says **about one hundred**.
- **Pittsburgh deaths:** commonly reported at about twenty on July 21 and another roughly twenty on July 22, with some counts higher. Student-facing copy says **about forty**.
- **Reading deaths:** published figures range from ten to sixteen. Student-facing copy says **at least ten**.
- **Homestead militia:** counts vary from roughly six thousand to eighty-five hundred. Student-facing copy says **roughly eight thousand**.
- **Pinkerton deaths at Homestead:** sources vary. The lesson uses **three**, matching the content master's conservative choice.
- **Allegheny County riot-loss liability:** the legal concept is real, but no unverified dollar figure is placed on the student page.

## Visual provenance policy

Narrative scene images for this lesson are planned separately in `assets.json`. Where strong historical visual evidence survives, production should begin from a verified period photograph, engraving, document, building, or artifact. When a direct visual source does not survive or does not fit the required composition, the page may use a clearly identified interpretive reconstruction. Generated or reconstructed scenes are never presented as archival photographs, and all readable historical language remains native HTML rather than fabricated inside images.
