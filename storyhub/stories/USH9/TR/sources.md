# Sources — Theodore Roosevelt

## Roosevelt, An Autobiography (1913), chapters I-II

Primary source. Retrospective memoir, chapters I–II. Roosevelt connects physical helplessness with his decision to train. His memory and self-presentation do not explain every later choice.

https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3335/pg3335-images.html

## Roosevelt, The Rough Riders (1899), chapters I and IV

Primary source. Participant memoir, chapters I and IV. His tally is 89 killed or wounded out of 490. A National Archives study gives 15 killed and 73 wounded. Neither tally means 89 deaths, and neither proves all losses were avoidable.

https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13000/pg13000-images.html

## Berryman, Drawing the Line in Mississippi (1902)

Primary source. Political cartoon, November 16, 1902. Evidence of the story’s public image, rather than a literal picture of the hunt. Berryman Family Papers, Archives of American Art, Smithsonian Institution.

https://www.archives.gov/exhibits/running-for-office/larger-image.php?TB_iframe=true&image=0.3

## Roosevelt, Through the Brazilian Wilderness (1914)

Primary source. Participant account. Search the text for the canoe injury, fever, doctor, and abscess. The boat photograph supplies expedition context; it does not document his sickest moment.

https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11746/pg11746-images.html

## Roosevelt, Grand Canyon remarks, May 6, 1903

Primary source. Public remarks at the Grand Canyon, May 6, 1903. Evidence of his argument for preservation and future generations.

https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/remarks-grand-canyon-arizona

## Roosevelt, Grand Canyon proclamation, January 11, 1908

Primary source. Official proclamation, January 11, 1908. Established a national monument. Congress established the national park in 1919.

https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/proclamation-794-setting-aside-grand-canyon-national-monument-arizona

## Scenes of Theodore Roosevelt at Sagamore Hill, 1912

Primary source · silent film. Pathé Frères; Library of Congress item mp76000076, cataloged by the Theodore Roosevelt Center. The exhibit uses source time 121–131 seconds: the end of a mail scene followed by Roosevelt walking with an axe. Resized to 960 × 720 while preserving the 4:3 frame. Original silent film; the soundscape and particles are editorial design.

https://www.theodorerooseveltcenter.org/digital-library/o284571/

## Opening film: archival images and editorial sound

Media production · primary-source images. Thirty-second opening, 1920 × 1080 at 24 fps. Authentic Pathé Frères film of Roosevelt at Sagamore Hill, 1912: source 121–127 seconds, followed by a held frame at 127 seconds. The age-eight portrait is Library of Congress item 2013651366. The quote “I was a sickly, delicate boy” comes from An Autobiography (1913), chapter I, a retrospective memoir. Whole-image movement, title typography, cinders, the score, and a Runway-generated projector texture are editorial additions. The archival film was silent; the sound does not record Roosevelt or his surroundings.

https://www.loc.gov/item/mp76000076/

## Accuracy and interpretation

- Childhood vulnerability helping drive adult training is an interpretation of Roosevelt’s retrospective memoir, not a complete psychological explanation.
- The Rough Riders were the 1st U.S. Volunteer Cavalry regiment. Most horses remained in Florida; some officers brought horses.
- Kettle Hill and San Juan Hill were separate heights. Regular Army units, including Black soldiers, shared the victory.
- Roosevelt’s 89 killed or wounded does not mean 89 killed. An Archives account gives 15 killed and 73 wounded; neither proves all losses were avoidable.
- Holt Collier tied an adult bear. Roosevelt refused an unsporting shot; the injured bear was subsequently killed. The toy story differs from the hunt.
- The River of Doubt lies in the Amazon basin; it was not the entire Amazon and Indigenous people lived in the region.
- Conservation is an explicitly dated epilogue returning to 1901–1909 after the 1914 climax. About 230 million acres includes multiple land categories; five national parks is the park count.
- Grand Canyon became a monument in 1908 and a park in 1919. The NPS was created in 1916 under Wilson. Protected land included Indigenous homelands.
