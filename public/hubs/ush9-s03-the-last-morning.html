The Last Morning

World War I was signed away a little after five in the morning. The guns kept firing until eleven. This is what happened in the six hours between.

About 40 minutes. Ten sections, a railroad car you can step inside, a clock you control, and three questions at the end.

No. 1Forest of Compiègne, France5:12 a.m.

The signatures

By November 1918, World War I had gone on for four years and three months. Around nine million soldiers were dead.

Germany was running out of men, food, and allies. Its leaders asked for an armistice, an agreement to stop fighting.

The meeting happened in a railroad car parked in a forest about forty miles north of Paris.

At about 5:12 in the morning on November 11, the Germans signed.

The fighting would stop at 11:00. Until then, the war was still on.

No. 3Conditions of the armisticeSigned 5:12 a.m.

What Germany agreed to

An armistice is not a peace treaty. It is a stop. The terms were written so Germany could not start again.

German troops had fifteen days to leave France, Belgium, Luxembourg, and Alsace Lorraine. Allied troops would occupy German land west of the Rhine River. The naval blockade that was starving Germany stayed in place.

Then Germany had to hand over the tools of war.

No. 4Allied headquartersTime chosen for the ceasefire

Why wait until 11?

The front ran more than four hundred miles. Word had to reach every army by telephone, telegraph, radio, and runners.

The hour was also easy to remember. The eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month.

Two days earlier, Foch had ordered his armies to keep up the pressure until the end. Nobody told them to stop attacking before 11:00.

Every commander knew the war would end at eleven. Each one decided what to do with the time that was left.

    No. 7Chaumont devant Damvillers, France10:59 a.m.

    Henry Gunther

    Henry Gunther was a bank clerk from Baltimore, drafted in September 1917. He went to France as a supply sergeant with the 313th Infantry, a regiment called Baltimore's Own.

    He wrote a letter home telling a friend how bad things were and warning him not to enlist. An Army censor read it. Gunther was busted down to private.

    On the last morning his unit was ordered forward near a French village. Two German machine gun teams blocked the road.

    The Germans knew the war had minutes left. Men who were there said the Germans tried to wave him back. Gunther kept charging with his bayonet.

    A burst of fire killed him at 10:59. One minute to go.

    Full story

    Gunther's regiment was part of the 79th Division. On the morning of November 11 the division was ordered to attack at 9:30, although the time of the armistice was by then common knowledge along the front.

    Why Gunther charged is not known. Some of the men with him believed he was trying to win back his rank. A Baltimore Sun reporter who talked to his platoon after the war told the story that way.

    General John J. Pershing's order of the day afterward named Gunther as the last American killed in the war. The Army restored his rank of sergeant after his death. His body came home to Baltimore in 1923.

    No. 9Every November 1111:00 a.m.

    From Armistice Day to Veterans Day

    When the guns stopped, men climbed out of the trenches and celebrated. Back home, people poured into the streets.

    November 11 became Armistice Day, a day to remember the war that was supposed to end all wars.

    It did not end all wars. So the day changed.

    1919

    President Woodrow Wilson marks the first Armistice Day.

    1921

    An unknown American soldier is buried at Arlington National Cemetery on November 11.

    1938

    Congress makes November 11 a national holiday.

    1954

    After World War II and Korea, the day is renamed Veterans Day to honor veterans of every war.

    The holiday still keeps the same hour. The eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month.
