    Chapter I · 1895 – Feb. 1898

    The Powder Keg

    Cubans fight for freedom. Two newspaper tycoons fight for readers. And a private letter goes very, very public.

    Cuban insurgents at Siboney, 1898 · Public domain

    Cuba Libre

    A war that started without us

    In 1895, Cuban rebels launched a war to free their island from Spain. Their leader, the poet and journalist José Martí, was killed in one of the first battles. The fighting went on without him.

    Spain sent General Valeriano Weyler to crush the rebellion. His plan, reconcentración, forced hundreds of thousands of rural Cubans into guarded camps so they couldn’t feed or hide the rebels. With little food, clean water, or medicine, well over 100,000 people died. American papers called him “Butcher” Weyler.

    Americans had money in the fight too. U.S. businesses had about $50 million invested in Cuba, mostly in sugar, and the war was wrecking it.

    “I have lived inside the monster, and I know its insides.”

    Martí, writing about the United States the day before he died, May 18, 1895. He feared the U.S. wanted Cuba for itself.

    The Yellow Kids

    Why “yellow” journalism?

    The name came from “The Yellow Kid,” a hit comic-strip character in a yellow nightshirt. Hearst and Pulitzer both ran versions of him, so critics named their screaming, sensational style after the Kid.

    In this 1898 cartoon, Pulitzer and Hearst are drawn as Yellow Kids, each shoving blocks that spell WAR. The cartoonist’s point: the papers were pushing the country toward a fight to sell copies.

    The Decision

    President William McKinley fought in the Civil War and had seen what battle does to people. He didn’t want this war. Now you’re in his chair.

    What really happened

    War, with a promise attached

    On April 11, McKinley asked Congress for power to end the fighting in Cuba. He mentioned Spain’s offer of a cease-fire only at the very end of his message.

    Congress demanded Spain leave Cuba and authorized force. It also passed the Teller Amendment: the U.S. would not take Cuba for itself. Spain cut off relations, and on April 25 Congress declared the two countries had been at war since April 21.

    The regular Army had only about 28,000 soldiers. More than 200,000 volunteers rushed to join.

    Meanwhile, in the Pacific

    The war’s first shots

    The first battle came 9,000 miles from Cuba. On May 1, Commodore George Dewey steamed into Manila Bay. He told his flagship’s captain, “You may fire when you are ready, Gridley.” Partway through, he paused the battle so his crews could eat breakfast. By lunch, every Spanish warship in the bay was sunk, burning, or captured.

    Weeks later the cruiser USS Charleston stopped at Guam on its way to Manila and fired at the old fort. Spanish officials rowed out to apologize for not returning the “salute.” No one had told them there was a war.

    Chapter IV · May – Sept. 1898

    Teddy & the Rough Riders

    “All men who feel any power of joy in battle know what it is like when the wolf rises in the heart.” Theodore Roosevelt, The Rough Riders, 1899

    Roosevelt and the Rough Riders on the hill they captured, 1898 · William Dinwiddie · Library of Congress

    The man behind the legend

    A desk job, then a cavalry charge

    In 1898, Theodore Roosevelt was 39, nearsighted, and the Assistant Secretary of the Navy. He had wanted a war with Spain for a long time.

    On February 25, ten days after the Maine exploded, his boss, Navy Secretary John Long, took an afternoon off. Roosevelt used it to cable Commodore Dewey: get the Pacific fleet ready to attack the Philippines. Long was furious.

    When war came, Roosevelt quit his job to fight. He became second-in-command of a brand-new volunteer cavalry regiment under his friend Colonel Leonard Wood. The newspapers named them the Rough Riders.

    “Roosevelt, in his precipitate way, has come very near causing more of an explosion than happened to the Maine.”

    Navy Secretary John D. Long, in his diary, February 1898

    The Buffalo Soldiers

    The soldiers the headlines left out

    The Rough Riders didn’t take the heights alone. Four Black regiments of the regular Army, the 9th and 10th Cavalry and the 24th and 25th Infantry, fought through the same fire. They were called Buffalo Soldiers, a name first given by Plains Indians decades earlier.

    Black soldiers fought for a country where, back home, many of them couldn’t vote or sit in the same train car as white passengers. Several earned the Medal of Honor in Cuba. Many newspaper stories still left them out.

    Two accounts · Who do you trust?

    Same Hill, Different Stories

    Theodore Roosevelt · The Rough Riders, 1899

    “They drifted to the rear”

    Roosevelt praised the Black troopers in 1898. A year later, in his best-selling book, he claimed some of them had started drifting back from the front line on San Juan Heights, and that he stopped them by drawing his revolver.

    Sgt. Presley Holliday, 10th Cavalry · New York Age, 1899

    “They were following orders”

    Holliday, who was there, wrote a public reply. He said the men had been ordered to the rear to carry wounded soldiers and bring up supplies, and that Roosevelt had misunderstood what he saw.

    Think: Roosevelt was famous and running for office. Holliday was an eyewitness with less power to be heard. How do a writer’s position and goals shape the story they tell?

    The great debate

    Imperialists vs. Anti-imperialists

    Imperialists wanted colonies for trade, naval bases, and world power. Some claimed it was America’s duty to “civilize” Filipinos, a belief rooted in racism.

    Anti-imperialists, including Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie, and Jane Addams, said that ruling people without their consent betrayed the Declaration of Independence. Some opposed annexation for racist reasons of their own.

    “I am opposed to having the eagle put its talons on any other land.”

    Mark Twain, New York Herald, October 1900

    The war after the war

    Freedom, postponed

    Filipino revolutionaries led by Emilio Aguinaldo had declared independence on June 12, 1898, and set up a congress at Malolos. They thought the Americans were allies. In August, U.S. and Spanish commanders secretly staged a short “battle” so Spain could surrender Manila to the Americans instead of the Filipinos.

    On February 4, 1899, fighting broke out between U.S. and Filipino troops. The Philippine-American War lasted over three years. U.S. forces burned villages and forced civilians into camps, much as Spain had done in Cuba. The Philippines did not become independent until July 4, 1946.

    Strings attached

    Did the U.S. keep its promise?

    The Teller Amendment promised not to take Cuba, and the U.S. didn’t annex it. But before U.S. troops left in 1902, Congress passed the Platt Amendment. It let the U.S. intervene in Cuba whenever it chose and required land for U.S. naval bases. Cuba had to write it into its own constitution.

    The Platt Amendment was canceled in 1934. One piece is still here: the U.S. naval base at Guantánamo Bay, under a lease that began in 1903. Cuba’s government calls it illegal and has refused to cash the rent checks for decades.
