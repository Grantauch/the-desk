BYTE 01 / 1945

THE WORLD WANTED ANSWERS.

World War II was over. The Nazi government had carried out mass murder, including the Holocaust, in which it murdered six million European Jews. The Allies had captured senior Nazi leaders.

Now what? Some leaders favored shooting Nazi officials without trials. Instead, four Allied countries set up an international court in Nuremberg, Germany. Accused leaders could have lawyers. Prosecutors had to present evidence.

The trials ended October 1, 1946. Of twelve death sentences, only ten were carried out.

Martin Bormann was tried in absentia—he was not there. Hermann Göring, a top Nazi leader, died by suicide using cyanide before his execution. The ten others were executed early on October 16.

Two scaffold platforms were used inside a prison gymnasium. A third was kept in reserve.

LOOK CLOSELY / 02

Where did the other two death sentences go?

Göring: died by suicide shortly before the executions.
Bormann: absent; the court sentenced him without him there. He had actually died in Berlin in 1945, though that was not then certain.

BYTE 03 / THE HANGMAN

MEET JOHN C. WOODS.

The U.S. Army assigned Master Sergeant John Clarence Woods to carry out the Nuremberg executions.

The press described a seasoned hangman: 43 years old, from Texas, with fifteen years of experience and 347 executions behind him.

Military historian French L. MacLean examined Woods’s records. The findings were different.

BYTE 04 / BEFORE DAWN · 16 OCTOBER

SOMETHING SOUNDED WRONG.

At about 1:11 a.m., Joachim von Ribbentrop arrived at the gallows. The last condemned man was hanged at about 2:45 a.m.

Correspondent Joseph Kingsbury-Smith witnessed the executions. He reported groans after Julius Streicher, a publisher of vicious antisemitic propaganda, dropped through the trapdoor.

But part of the drop space was screened off from witnesses. He could report the sounds he heard, not everything happening out of sight.

WITNESS / JOSEPH KINGSBURY-SMITH

DIRECT OBSERVATION: A reporter heard distressing sounds.

BYTE 06 / FOUR YEARS LATER

THE HANGMAN’S OWN END.

John Woods survived the war and the Nuremberg executions. In July 1950, at age 39, he died in an electrical accident on Eniwetok Atoll in the Marshall Islands.

Later stories claimed he was killed while building an electric chair—or even murdered by German scientists. Reliable evidence does not establish either story.

The accident was real. The elaborate conspiracy stories are not supported.
