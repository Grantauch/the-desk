Burn the Files

In January 1973 the man who ran the CIA was weeks from leaving. The record of MKULTRA went first.

152 files. The operational record of MKULTRA, the paper that says who was tested, where and how.

Why burn them

Richard Helms had been Director of Central Intelligence since 1966. President Nixon had decided to replace him, and he would leave in February 1973. Senator Edward Kennedy said at the 1977 hearing that Helms ordered the MKULTRA files destroyed. Technical Services staff carried it out on Sidney Gottlieb's instruction. Gottlieb retired that same year.

Helms later said the program was over, the papers could be misunderstood, and the outside researchers had been promised secrecy. Critics point to the timing. That same January the Watergate burglars went on trial, and Washington was starting to dig into what its government had hidden. Both explanations can be partly true. The effect is certain. When investigators came in 1975, the operational record was gone.

WHY A MISSING FILE PROVES NOTHING EITHER WAY

Say your phone records get wiped. Someone claims you texted a threat last spring. Can they prove it? No, the record's gone. Can you prove you didn't? Also no. A missing record doesn't make the claim true, and it doesn't make it false. It just moves the question somewhere else, to witnesses, to other copies, to whatever survived.

That's the position every MKULTRA investigator has been in since 1973. People who want the worst version to be true say the burning proves a cover up of everything. People who want to defend the CIA say there's no evidence. Both are using a hole in the record as if it were evidence. The honest move is harder. You say exactly what the surviving paper shows, and you say exactly where it stops.

August 3, 1977

Six, not 149.

Turner went through the recovered files subproject by subproject. Then he said the most important sentence of the hearing: There are 6 cases of these 149 where we have enough evidence in this new documentation to substantiate that there was unwitting testing. Each square below is one subproject. Press each filter. Then press the last two and compare them.

That does not say there was not, but we have no such evidence.
Adm. Stansfield Turner, asked whether the White House approved MKULTRA, August 3, 1977
The smallest verdict the record earns

Sort the claims.

Here is the test for this whole unit. Pick a claim, then pick the bin where the evidence puts it. Confirmed means the record proves it. Unproven means it could be true but the record cannot show it. Unsupported means the record gives no basis for it. Misleading means it twists something real.

Pick a claim first.

June 30, 2026

Back in the hearing room.

Almost 49 years later, a House Oversight task force chaired by Representative Anna Paulina Luna of Florida held a hearing called Mind Control and Accountability. Witness Tom O'Neill called it the first congressional hearing on MKULTRA since 1977. What the witnesses said is testimony. Testimony is evidence that someone made a claim, so each one is labeled below.

Witness

Stephen Kinzer

Author of Poisoner in Chief, a 2019 biography of Gottlieb. Testified that MKULTRA ran the most extreme experiments on humans ever carried out by a U.S. agency, and that the number of victims is unknown because the files were destroyed in 1973.

Second half matches the record. First half is his judgment.
Witness

Tom O'Neill

Author of CHAOS. Alleged that passages were removed from CIA documents before they went to Congress in 1977, so the old investigation worked from an incomplete record.

Allegation. Needs the original documents to check.
Both

The ask

End redactions on documents already released, and search again for records that were never turned over. Kinzer warned that modern neuroscience and artificial intelligence could make a successor program far more capable.

A warning about the future, not a finding about the past.
