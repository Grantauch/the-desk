Joe Louis and the Weight of a Fight
Detroit 1926·New York 1938·Arlington 1981

On June 22, 1938, a heavyweight championship fight lasted 124 seconds.

About 70,000 people were inside Yankee Stadium and around 70 million more were listening on the radio. The winner was a 24 year old Black man from Detroit, and that night white America cheered for him. This investigation asks what the country wanted from Joe Louis, and what it gave him back.

The RulesDetroit, 1926 to 1937

Joe Louis Barrow was born in 1914 to Alabama sharecroppers. In 1926 the family moved to Detroit, where he worked at Ford's River Rouge plant and learned to box at the Brewster Recreation Center.

Every Black fighter of his time boxed in the shadow of Jack Johnson, the first Black heavyweight champion. Johnson grinned at the white men he beat and married white women. The novelist Jack London wrote that someone had to remove that smile from Johnson's face. After Johnson lost the title in 1915, no Black fighter got a title shot for 22 years.

Louis's managers, two Black businessmen named John Roxborough and Julian Black, did not want a second Johnson. The Smithsonian lists four of the rules they gave him.

The RulebookSort each rule

For each rule, decide what it was really about.

Never be photographed with a white woman

Nothing here touches a fight. Johnson's marriages to white women were a main reason white newspapers attacked him.

Never gloat over a fallen opponent

It happens in the ring, but it is aimed at the audience. Johnson's smile is what London wanted wiped away.

Never engage in fixed fights

This is the rule most about boxing itself. A fixed fight would also have handed his critics an easy story.

Live clean and fight clean

Half ring, half everything else. His managers wanted a champion no newspaper could use against Black fighters.

B
The First FightJune 19, 1936 · Yankee Stadium

Louis was 22 and had won all 27 of his professional fights. Max Schmeling was 30, a former champion from Germany. Schmeling had studied films of Louis and noticed that he dropped his left hand after every jab. He knocked Louis down in the fourth round and knocked him out in the twelfth.

The Nazi government, which taught that Germans were a master race, sold the win as proof. Schmeling never joined the Nazi Party and kept his Jewish American manager.

I am a fighter, not a politician.

Max Schmeling

On June 22, 1937, Louis knocked out James Braddock in Chicago to become the first Black heavyweight champion since Johnson. He told reporters he did not want to be called champion until he beat Schmeling.

C
The RematchJune 22, 1938 · Yankee Stadium

By 1938 Germany had taken over Austria, and the rematch was about more than a title. Louis later said President Roosevelt felt his arm at the White House and told him America needed muscles like his to beat Germany.

A crowd of 70,043 paid more than $1 million to get in. Schmeling went down three times, and the referee stopped the fight at two minutes and four seconds of the first round. Schmeling had thrown two punches.

D
The Bill1942 to 1981

A month after Pearl Harbor, on January 9, 1942, Louis gave his purse from a title fight to the Navy Relief Society. At the time the Navy let Black men serve only as cooks and mess attendants. The next day he enlisted in a segregated Army. On March 10 he spoke at a Navy Relief show at Madison Square Garden, and the government turned his words into a recruiting poster. The words come down to us in two versions.

One Sentence, Two VersionsMarch 1942
As reported from the Garden that nightI'm only doin' what any red blooded American would. We're gonna do our part and we'll win 'cause we are on God's side.
As printed on the government posterWe're going to do our part . . . and we'll win because we're on God's side.

He gave a second purse to Army Emergency Relief, fought about 100 exhibitions for troops, and rose to staff sergeant. Then the bill came.
