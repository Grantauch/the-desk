Loaded with Dynamite

January 8, 1918. The war still had ten months to run. Woodrow Wilson told Congress what the peace should look like.

Wilson read a list of fourteen points to a joint session of Congress. He called it "the only possible programme" for peace.

Two months earlier, the new Bolshevik government in Russia had published the secret treaties the Allies signed with each other, deals that promised land to the winners. Wilson wanted to show the world that America was fighting for something different. On January 8, 1918 he went before both houses of Congress and laid out fourteen points. Some were about the whole world, like open agreements between countries and freedom of the seas. Others named places, like Belgium, Alsace Lorraine and Poland. The last point called for a new association of nations to keep the peace.

Address to Congress / January 8, 1918

"The programme of the world's peace, therefore, is our programme; and that programme, the only possible programme, as we see it, is this:"

Woodrow Wilson, address to Congress, January 8, 1918. Avalon Project, Yale Law School.

The war ended ten months later on a railroad siding in France. That morning has its own hub: The Last Morning.

Section 2

The fourteen points

Fourteen points. Six were rules for every country. Eight named a place on the map.

The general points asked every nation to change how it behaved, winners included. The place points mostly redrew borders in Europe.

Wilson's first five points were rules. Make agreements in public. Keep the seas open to all ships. Lower trade barriers. Cut armies and navies. Settle colonial claims fairly and give the colonized people's interests equal weight. Points six through thirteen named places, most of them land held by Germany, Austria Hungary or the Ottoman Empire. Point fourteen, the last, was the association of nations.

Section 3

One phrase

Wilson never used the word in January. Five weeks later he did, and it went around the world.

"Self-determination" means a people choosing its own government. Wilson's own Secretary of State thought the phrase was dangerous.

The phrase self-determination is not in the January speech. On February 11, 1918, Wilson used it in another address to Congress and said statesmen would ignore it "at their peril." The US government's war information office printed Wilson's speeches and sent them around the world by telegraph and in pamphlets. Allied planes dropped copies over German lines. Newspapers from Cairo to Shanghai printed them. Readers there knew that Wilson spoke mostly about Europe. Many decided the principle had to apply to them too.

Address to Congress / February 11, 1918

"'Self-determination' is not a mere phrase. It is an imperative principle of action, which statesmen will henceforth ignore at their peril."

Woodrow Wilson, address to Congress, February 11, 1918.

Confidential
Private notes / December 30, 1918

"The phrase is simply loaded with dynamite. It will raise hopes which can never be realized."

Robert Lansing, US Secretary of State, private notes, December 30, 1918.

Section 4

Paris

December 14, 1918. No American president had ever gone to Europe while in office. Paris came out to meet him.

Wilson went to the peace conference himself and stayed for most of six months. Europe greeted him as the man who would make a fair peace.

Wilson sailed from New York on the liner George Washington on December 4, 1918 and landed at Brest, France on December 13. The next day he rode through Paris with French President Raymond Poincare. Newspapers estimated the crowd at around two million people. He was the first sitting president to travel to Europe. Apart from one trip home in February and March, he stayed in France until the end of June 1919. The American delegation set up its offices in the Hotel de Crillon, on the Place de la Concorde.

Section 5

Four men in a room

Four men made most of the decisions. Each one came to Paris carrying a different number of dead.

The Big Four were Wilson, Georges Clemenceau of France, David Lloyd George of Britain and Vittorio Orlando of Italy. France had lost far more men than America had, and its leader wanted Germany too weak to attack again.

Delegates from about thirty countries came to Paris, but the big decisions were made in private by four leaders, later called the Council of Four. They met more than 140 times between March and June 1919, often in Wilson's study, with no reporters. Clemenceau was 77. He had seen Germany invade France twice in his lifetime, in 1870 and in 1914. Lloyd George had just won an election in December 1918 where voters wanted Germany to pay for the war. Orlando wanted the land the Allies had promised Italy in a secret treaty in 1915. When the others refused to hand Italy the port of Fiume, Orlando walked out of the conference in April 1919.

Estimates vary by source. These are common round figures for soldiers killed. Civilian deaths are not included.

Section 6

The letters at the Crillon

People who were not invited wrote to Wilson anyway. They quoted his own words back to him.

Groups from colonies and occupied countries sent petitions and delegates to Paris asking for self-determination. Europe's new nations got it. Almost no one outside Europe did.

Poland, Czechoslovakia and the new kingdom that became Yugoslavia were all recognized as countries in 1918 and 1919, along with Finland, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania. Each had been ruled by one of the empires that lost the war or collapsed during it. People living under the winners' empires, or under Japan, asked for the same principle. Four of their stories are on the map.

Section 7

Eleven of seventeen

April 11, 1919. A majority voted yes. Wilson ruled that it did not pass.

Japan asked that the League of Nations covenant state the equality of nations and fair treatment of their citizens regardless of race. Most of the committee agreed. Wilson, chairing the committee, ruled that it needed every vote.

Japan's delegate, Baron Makino Nobuaki, proposed adding a line about the equality of nations and the just treatment of their people to the League's founding document. Japanese immigrants faced unequal laws in the United States, Canada and Australia. Australia's prime minister, Billy Hughes, fought the proposal hard, and the British delegation would not support it. On April 11, 1919, the League of Nations Commission voted. Eleven of the seventeen members present voted yes. Wilson was chairing the meeting. He ruled that a question this serious needed unanimous agreement, so the proposal failed.

Section 8

The Hall of Mirrors

June 28, 1919. Five years to the day after the shots in Sarajevo, Germany signed.

The Treaty of Versailles took land from Germany, shrank its military, and made it accept responsibility for the war. The League of Nations was written into the first part of the treaty.

The signing took place in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles, the same room where the German Empire had been proclaimed in 1871 after it beat France. Germany lost about 13 percent of its land in Europe and about 10 percent of its people. Alsace Lorraine went back to France. A strip of land gave Poland a path to the sea and cut Germany in two. All of Germany's colonies were taken. Its army was capped at 100,000 men, with no air force and no submarines. Article 231 said Germany and its allies were responsible for the loss and damage of the war. In 1921 the Allies set the bill for reparations at 132 billion gold marks.

Treaty of Versailles / Article 231

"The Allied and Associated Governments affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies."

Article 231, Treaty of Versailles, June 28, 1919. Avalon Project, Yale Law School.

Section 10

Words you have already seen

Four words from this story. You have seen each one at work.

Self-determination
A people choosing its own government. It is the phrase Lansing called dynamite, and the reason Korea, Vietnam, Egypt and China wrote to Paris.
League of Nations
The association of nations in point XIV, written into the treaty to settle disputes before they became wars.
Mandate
A territory taken from the losers and run by a winning country. It is what happened to Germany's colonies and the Ottoman Arab lands.
Reparations
Payments a defeated country makes for war damage. Germany's bill was set in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks.
