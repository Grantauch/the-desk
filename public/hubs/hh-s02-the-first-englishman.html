Hidden History · Specimen file · 1912 to 1953

The First Englishman

For forty one years the most important human fossil in Britain sat in a museum drawer. It was a fake the whole time.

How does a forgery fool the experts, and what does it break while it is believed?

About 40 minutes. Eleven drawers, three tests, one specimen you can take apart, and three questions at the end.

Drawer I February 1912

The gravel pit

A country solicitor named Charles Dawson wrote to the Natural History Museum in London.

He said workmen digging gravel in Sussex had turned up pieces of a very old human skull, and that he had been collecting them.

The letter went to Arthur Smith Woodward, the Keeper of Geology, one of the most respected scientists in Britain.

Woodward went down to Sussex and started digging with him.

Charles DawsonSolicitor and amateur antiquarian from Sussex. Fellow of the Geological Society. Known for real finds before this one.
Arthur Smith WoodwardKeeper of Geology at the Natural History Museum. About as strong an institutional name as existed.
Full story

Charles Dawson was a solicitor in Sussex and a well known amateur antiquarian. He had made real finds before, he corresponded with professionals, and he was a Fellow of the Geological Society. Amateur collectors mattered enormously in this period, because most fossils are found by people who are already outdoors for another reason.

In February 1912 he wrote to Woodward about skull fragments recovered from a gravel pit at Piltdown, in Sussex.

The two of them dug at the site together across that summer. They recovered further pieces of a braincase, a jawbone with two molars still in it, and some worked flints and animal bones that suggested a very early date.

The braincase was thick and large. The jaw was heavy and ape like. Taken together the pieces described a creature with a modern sized brain and a primitive jaw, which is exactly the order most scientists at the time expected.

Drawer II December 18, 1912

The announcement

Woodward and Dawson presented the find to the Geological Society of London.

They said it was a human ancestor, perhaps five hundred thousand years old.

It was given a scientific name, Eoanthropus dawsoni, meaning Dawson's dawn man.

The newspapers had a better name for it. The first Englishman.

Full story

The presentation took place at a meeting of the Geological Society of London on December 18, 1912. The room was full.

Woodward described a creature combining a large modern braincase with a primitive jaw, and proposed that it represented an early stage of human evolution. Naming it for Dawson was ordinary practice.

Coverage in the British press was immediate and enormous. The framing was national. Britain now had its own ancestor, found in Sussex, and the phrase the first Englishman ran everywhere. Human origins in this period were a matter of national standing as well as science.

Not everyone in the room agreed. Objections were raised at the meeting itself, mostly about whether the jaw and the braincase belonged to the same individual, since they had been found separately and the parts that would connect them were missing. Woodward's reconstruction answered the objection by simply assuming the jaw and the braincase came from one creature.

Drawer III Why it was believed

It answered the question they were asking

Scientists in 1912 expected the brain to have grown first, before the jaw and the teeth became human.

Piltdown had a large brain and an ape's jaw.

They also expected their own country to have an ancestor, because the neighbors already did.

The expectation in 1912

Open a find and read how fast it was accepted.

What an ancestor was expected to look like

  • The brain came first. Brain growth came before human jaws, teeth, and posture.
  • A mosaic. A modern braincase carried on a primitive body.

What each country already had

  • Germany: Neanderthal, 1856, and the Mauer jaw, 1907.
  • France: Cro Magnon, 1868, and La Chapelle, 1908.
  • Britain: nothing of comparable importance. Britain was overdue.

Choose a find.

None of these finds was assessed by a different standard on paper. The standard was the same. What changed was how hard people looked.

The find that fit best was accepted fastest. The find that fit worst was correct.

Full story

Two expectations were in the room. The first was anatomical. The leading view held that the defining human feature was the brain, and that brain growth came first, with the jaw, the teeth, and posture following. A fossil with a big braincase and a primitive jaw was the predicted in between creature. Piltdown was not a surprise. It was a confirmation.

The second was national. Fossil human remains had been turning up across Europe for half a century. Germany had the Neanderthal skeleton from 1856 and a very early jaw from Mauer in 1907. France had the Cro Magnon remains from 1868 and a well studied Neanderthal from La Chapelle in 1908. Britain, which considered itself at the front of the sciences, had nothing of comparable importance.

Neither expectation is shameful. The brain first idea was a reasonable reading of the evidence then available. National interest in a find is human and it is still with us. What they did together was lower the bar. A find that satisfied both was easier to accept than a find that satisfied neither, and nobody had to lie for that to happen.

Drawer IV The people who said no

It was not unanimous

In 1915 an American zoologist compared the jaw to an orangutan's and said it was an ape's.

A French authority on fossil humans said the same thing.

Others said the skull and the jaw simply could not be one animal.

They were right, they published, and it did not matter.

Full story

Gerrit Smith Miller, a zoologist at the Smithsonian in Washington, examined casts of the Piltdown material and published in 1915. His conclusion was that the braincase was human and the jaw belonged to an ape, and he argued it directly from comparison with orangutan anatomy.

Marcellin Boule in France, the leading authority on fossil humans of his generation, reached a similar view. Franz Weidenreich, who later worked on the Chinese fossil material, said flatly that the jaw was an orangutan's and the skull a modern human's.

These objections did not disappear. They were published, cited, and available for four decades. But the objectors were outside Britain, the real specimens were rarely made available for direct study, most work was done from plaster casts, and the weight of the institution sat on one side.

A cast keeps the shape and loses almost everything a chemical or microscope test would need.

Drawer V 1915 to 1917

The second site

Dawson told Woodward he had found more material at a second location a couple of miles away.

Dawson died in August 1916.

Woodward announced the second site in 1917, and it settled the argument.

A find at one place can be a mistake. The same creature at two independent places is a fact.

Full story

Dawson reported to Woodward that he had recovered fragments at a second site, about two miles from the original pit. The material included pieces of a braincase and a molar tooth. Dawson died in August 1916. Woodward announced the second find in 1917.

The effect was decisive. The most serious objection to Piltdown had always been that the skull and the jaw might be two animals thrown together by chance in one gravel bed. A second occurrence of the same combination somewhere else made coincidence hard to believe. Doubt in Britain largely stopped.

Nobody except Dawson was ever shown the exact location of that second site, and no further material was recovered there by anyone else.

In 2016 a research team ran DNA analysis on the teeth from both sites. The canine from the first site and the molar from the second came from the same orangutan.

Two sites. One animal. One person who knew where both of them were.

Drawer VI Forty one years

What got built on it

  • Reconstructions of the head, modeled and reprinted for decades.
  • A place in the family tree in textbook after textbook.
  • A museum display in London.
  • Research careers and papers about a creature that did not exist.
  • A national monument at the gravel pit, declared in 1950. Three years before the exposure.

Drawer VII 1949

A test that did not exist in 1912

Kenneth Oakley at the Natural History Museum was working on a chemical method for dating bone.

Bone buried in the ground absorbs fluorine from groundwater. The longer it sits, the more it holds.

He ran it on Piltdown.

The result was in the region of fifty thousand years, not five hundred thousand.

Full story

Kenneth Oakley was a geologist at the Natural History Museum. He was developing the fluorine absorption method, which works because bone lying in the ground takes up fluorine from groundwater over time. Two bones from the same deposit that have been there the same length of time should carry similar fluorine levels.

In 1949 he applied it to the Piltdown material. The results showed the remains were far younger than claimed. That was not yet a charge of forgery. It meant Piltdown was not the ancient ancestor it had been presented as, which was already a serious problem for its place in the family tree.

The finding brought closer attention. Joseph Weiner at Oxford began working through the material with Wilfrid Le Gros Clark and with Oakley, and the question shifted from how old is it to what is it.

Full story

Joseph Weiner worked through the problem with Wilfrid Le Gros Clark and with Kenneth Oakley. The results were published in 1953.

The braincase was human and it was not ancient. The jaw was an ape's, and the identification pointed to an orangutan.

The teeth had been filed. Under a microscope the wear surfaces carried straight parallel scratches, which is what a file leaves and not what chewing leaves. Chewing wear runs in the varied directions of real use.

The material had been stained. The color that made it look ancient had been applied rather than picked up from the ground. Some of the animal bones and worked flints at the site had been planted as well, and several had been treated the same way.

The announcement was made publicly in November 1953 and it was a considerable scandal.

Drawer IX 2016

One forger

A research team ran the whole collection again with modern methods.

The teeth from both sites came from a single orangutan.

At least two human specimens were used, possibly medieval.

The same putty appears throughout, holding gravel plugs in the cavities.

One consistent method across every piece at both sites. One person had access to both.

Full story

A study published in 2016, led by Isabelle De Groote at Liverpool John Moores University with Chris Stringer of the Natural History Museum among the authors, examined every piece again. The methods included precise measurement, three dimensional imaging, CT scanning, chemical analysis, and DNA.

A single orangutan supplied the jaw and the teeth, and DNA linked the canine from the first site and the molar from the second to the same animal. At least two human specimens supplied the skull pieces. Cavities had been packed with gravel from the Piltdown sites and plugged with small pebbles, and the same putty was used throughout.

That consistency is the finding. One recipe, applied the same way to every piece, across both locations.

Charles Dawson is the only person known to have been connected to both sites. The study concludes that a single forger is most likely and that Dawson is the most likely candidate. Many other people have been proposed over the years, including Woodward and the writer Arthur Conan Doyle, who lived nearby. None of those proposals rests on physical evidence of the kind the 2016 work produced.

Dawson's motive is not established. He was seeking membership in the Royal Society and had other finds that were later questioned. That is suggestive. It is not proof.

Drawer X 1925

The fossil that was real

In South Africa, a young anatomist named Raymond Dart described a fossil skull of a child.

It had a small brain and human teeth, and the opening for the spinal cord sat where an upright walker's would.

It was genuine. It was the first of its kind ever found. It reversed the expected order, because the body had become human before the brain did.

Most of the field ignored it for about twenty years.

Piltdown was a big part of why.

Raymond DartAnatomist in Johannesburg. He was thirty two when his paper appeared in the journal Nature, and lived to see the field agree with him. Pictured later in life. Smithsonian Institution Archives.
Full story

Raymond Dart was an anatomist working in Johannesburg. In 1924 a fossil skull came to him from a limeworks at Taung. He described it in Nature in February 1925 and named it Australopithecus africanus.

The specimen was a child with a small braincase, teeth that were human rather than ape like, and a spinal opening placed as it is in creatures that walk upright. Every part of that contradicted the expectation from Drawer III. The brain was small, so the brain had not come first. And it was in Africa, which was not where most of the field was looking.

The reception was largely dismissive. Leading figures argued it was a young ape. Acceptance came slowly from the late 1940s as more fossils like it turned up, and then decisively after 1953.

Piltdown was not the only reason. Dart's paper was brief, a child's skull is genuinely harder to read, and attention was fixed on Europe and Asia. But a forged fossil had defined what a correct answer was supposed to look like, and so the correct answer looked wrong.

Drawer XI What caught it

Not a confession

Nobody admitted anything. No document turned up. No witness came forward.

It was caught by a scientist measuring fluorine for another reason, and then by a microscope pointed at a tooth.

Charles Dawson died in 1916 and was never questioned about any of it.

The record does not correct itself. People with instruments correct it, and only when they look.
