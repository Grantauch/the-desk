byte 01

A test the city did not choose

In September 1950, the U.S. military released bacteria from a vessel off the San Francisco coast to investigate vulnerability to biological attack. The test became known as Operation Sea-Spray. Residents did not receive a chance to opt in. This is a documented secret operation, not merely a rumor about ordinary fog. The fog in our illustration is atmosphere; it does not show visible bacteria or the exact test conditions. S1, S2

byte 02

Why use a living tracer?

The military used Serratia marcescens, then treated by planners as suitable for a simulation, to trace dispersal. Monitoring could show whether material released offshore reached populated areas. A tracer is something used to follow a pathway. Here the pathway was air movement. A successful dispersal measurement answers “where did it travel?” It does not, by itself, answer whether every exposed person became ill. S1, S2

byte 03

Then came an illness claim

A hospital outbreak followed, and Edward Nevin died after an infection. His family later argued that the military release caused his death. Timing and a shared bacterial species made the question serious, but they did not automatically identify the source of a particular infection. Our evidence ladder separates a release, possible exposure, infection and an established causal link. Each step requires additional evidence. Do not treat the death as a proven consequence of the test. S1, S2

The court’s ruling is not a laboratory result

Majority, 1983

Government discretionary-function immunity blocked this legal action. The court ordered jurisdictional dismissal.

Dissent, 1983

Would reject that immunity reasoning, but accept the lower court’s finding against the claimed causal link.

byte 04

The appellate court’s actual question

In 1983 the Ninth Circuit majority directed dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, applying a legal exception protecting discretionary government policy decisions. Put simply, it held that this suit could not proceed under that legal route. That is different from a scientific demonstration that the test was harmless. The dissent disagreed about the immunity issue but would have accepted the trial court’s finding that the Army bacteria did not cause Nevin’s death. Read those positions separately. S2

byte 05

Ask an ethical question that the evidence can carry

You do not have to prove a particular death to ask whether people should have been exposed without consent. You also do not get to settle a causal question simply because the secrecy was troubling. Historical judgment can recognize a documented breach of public trust while keeping uncertainty about a specific outcome. The evidence exercise makes you identify which question an item can answer before you use it as proof.

byte 06

Do not turn one case into every cloud

Sea-Spray establishes a specific operation at a specific place and time. Other military dispersion tests used other materials and have their own records; their findings cannot be pasted into this one. Evidence for a real past release does not establish a claim about any unexplained present-day cloud. A strong historical claim names the event, names the source and stops where the evidence stops. A secondary overview of the September 1950 release, monitoring and a later hospital outbreak. Its broad causal suggestions require comparison with the court record.

Context about the wider history of dispersion testing. Zinc cadmium sulfide tests used different materials from Sea-Spray; never merge their health findings into this case.
