Project Azorian · 1968 to 1975

The Ship That Wasn't Mining

In 1974 the CIA disguised a spy ship as a Howard Hughes mining venture and sent it to pull a sunken Soviet nuclear submarine off the floor of the Pacific, three miles down, while Soviet ships watched.

The case question

How did the CIA try to steal a submarine from three miles down, and why will it still neither confirm nor deny what it found?

Byte 1

March 1968

A submarine disappears

The Soviet submarine K-129 left its base at Petropavlovsk around March 1, 1968, carrying 98 men and three nuclear missiles. About a week later it sank somewhere in the middle of the Pacific. Nobody aboard got off a distress call.

The Soviet Navy searched for two months and found nothing. What caused the sinking is still unknown.

The Americans had a better idea of where to look. Navy underwater microphones had recorded the sound of the disaster. That summer the spy submarine USS Halibut towed cameras over the area and photographed the wreck lying on the bottom, about 1,560 miles northwest of Hawaii.

1969 to 1974

Three miles of pipe

CIA engineers proposed something nobody had ever done. A ship would lower a giant steel claw on a string of pipe and haul the submarine straight up. The CIA's estimate of the weight was about 2,000 to 2,200 tons.

Byte 3

1972 to 1973

Enter Howard Hughes

A secret this big needed a cover story. Howard Hughes was a billionaire famous for hiding from the public, and his companies already did secret work for the government. A senior Hughes official asked him to lend his name, and he agreed.

The world was told Hughes was building a ship to scoop metal nodules off the deep ocean floor. Mining experts believed it. Some companies started planning ocean mining ventures of their own.

The claw itself was built out of sight inside a giant covered barge called HMB-1. Off Catalina Island the barge sank itself, and the ship parked on top and pulled the claw up through doors in the bottom of its own hull.

Chazhma left that night. Four days later a 155 foot Soviet salvage tug called SB-10 arrived and stayed for weeks. August 1 to 9, 1974

The claw breaks

The claw closed on the submarine and lifted it off the bottom on August 1. Partway up, some of its steel grabbing arms failed. Most of the hull broke away and fell back three miles to the seafloor.

On August 8 what was left reached the ship. Tap each part of K-129 to see what happened to it.

Radiation monitors aboard picked up plutonium leaking from the recovered nuclear torpedoes. Exactly what came up is still classified. Accounts disagree on whether any code machines were inside, and the CIA has never said.

Byte 6

September 4, 1974

Six sailors

Inside the recovered section were the bodies of six Soviet sailors. On September 4, 1974, the Americans buried them at sea in a steel chamber about 90 miles southwest of Hawaii. The coffins were covered with the Soviet naval flag. A chaplain read prayers, and the Soviet national anthem played.

The CIA filmed the ceremony. For 18 years the families were told nothing. In October 1992, after the Soviet Union collapsed, CIA Director Robert Gates flew to Moscow and handed the film to Russian President Boris Yeltsin.

One dot for each man aboard K-129. Six were ever recovered.

Byte 7

June 1974 to March 1975

The burglary that blew the cover

  1. June 1974
    Burglars break into Summa Corporation headquarters in Los Angeles, a Hughes company, and haul away files. The CIA later feared one was a memo to Hughes describing the submarine plan.
  2. February 1975
    The Los Angeles Times prints a confused version of the story connecting Hughes and his mining ship to the CIA.
  3. Feb to March 1975
    CIA Director William Colby personally asks newspaper editors to hold the story. Several of the biggest papers in the country agree.
  4. March 1975
    Columnist Jack Anderson breaks it on national radio and TV. The next day The New York Times runs Seymour Hersh's story on its front page.

With the cover blown, the White House canceled a second mission that would have gone back for the rest of the submarine.

Byte 8

March 1975 to today

Neither confirm nor deny

Rolling Stone reporter Harriet Ann Phillippi filed a Freedom of Information Act request for CIA records about the ship. A CIA lawyer wrote an answer built so that even a no could not tip off the Soviets.

"We can neither confirm nor deny the existence of the information requested but, hypothetically, if such data were to exist, the subject matter would be classified, and could not be disclosed."The CIA's reply to Phillippi, 1975

A federal appeals court upheld the answer in 1976. In 1977 the Carter administration admitted the project existed. Government agencies still give the same kind of answer today, and it is still called a Glomar response.

Your verdict

Fifty years later, still a white box

The mission ended in 1974. The Soviet Union is gone. The CIA's own history of Project Azorian still has whole paragraphs blanked out, and agencies still answer requests the way the CIA answered Harriet Ann Phillippi.
