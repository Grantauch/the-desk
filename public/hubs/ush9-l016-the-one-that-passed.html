01JUNE 1867

THE SIERRA

Three thousand workers walked off the Central Pacific Railroad high in the Sierra Nevada.

They wanted the day cut from eleven hours to ten, shorter shifts inside the tunnels, and forty dollars a month.

Charles Crocker stopped the supply wagons. All of them.

Eight days later they went back.

10HOURS A DAY
$40A MONTH
8DAYS UNTIL RETURN
The core idea +

Nineteen years before Haymarket, three thousand Chinese workers made the same demand American labor would build a movement around, and they were already part of the labor struggle. They lost it in a week because their employer controlled the road that carried their food. This is who these workers were before any law was written about them.

Read the full story +

By early 1867 the Central Pacific employed more than eleven thousand Chinese workers, roughly nine in ten of everyone on the line. They cut the grade, set the black powder, and drove the tunnels through granite at Donner Summit. They earned between one half and two thirds of what white workers earned, and unlike white workers they bought their own food out of that pay.

On June 24 they stopped. The demand was a raise to forty dollars a month, a reduction of the workday from eleven hours to ten, and shorter shifts for the men working inside the tunnels, where the danger was worst. Some accounts add a demand that the overseers stop beating them.

Crocker did not negotiate. He cut off the supply wagons carrying provisions into the mountains, arranged for armed men to be available, and let the camps sit. There was no violence and no sabotage. The men stayed in their camps and did not work. After eight days, with no food coming up the road, they went back on the company's terms.

Crocker later described the strike this way.

SOURCE TEXT

This strike of the Chinese was just like Sunday all along the work.
Charles Crocker, on the June 1867 strike

Wages for experienced Chinese workers went up quietly some time afterward. Nothing was announced and nothing was signed.

02JULY 23, 1877

THE SANDLOT

Eight thousand San Francisco workers gathered near City Hall to support the railroad strikers back east.

The speakers refused to blame Chinese immigrants. The crowd kept shouting for it anyway.

By the third night, four people were dead and twenty Chinese laundries had been burned.

A wagon driver named Denis Kearney helped put the riot down. Three months later he built a political party on it.

“The Chinese
must go.”

Denis Kearney · Workingmen’s Party of California · 1877

The core idea +

This is the same July students already watched in Lecture 15. The Great Railroad Strike reached San Francisco as a sympathy rally and turned into three nights of attacks on Chinese homes and businesses. The man who came out of it with a movement had been on the other side of the line, carrying a pick handle for the committee that broke up the riot.

Read the full story +

The rally on the evening of July 23 was called by the Workingmen's Party of the United States, which had been reading the same telegraph reports out of Pittsburgh and Baltimore that had lit up eleven other cities that week. Roughly eight thousand people came. The organizers had promised the meeting would not be about the Chinese. From the crowd came shouts to talk about the Chinese anyway. A group of perhaps two hundred young men broke off from the meeting and went into the nearby Chinese quarter.

The violence ran three nights. Four people were killed. Around twenty Chinese owned laundries were destroyed, and the Chinese Methodist Mission was damaged. On the second night a crowd moved on the Pacific Mail Steamship docks, the pier where Chinese immigrants arrived, and settled for burning a lumberyard at the Beale Street Wharf when they found the docks guarded.

The guarding was done by a Committee of Safety under the merchant William T. Coleman, which put somewhere between one thousand and four thousand armed volunteers into the streets carrying hickory pick handles, alongside two hundred fifty police and twelve hundred state militia. Denis Kearney, an Irish immigrant who drove a wagon for a living, served in that pick handle brigade.

He then tried to join the Workingmen's Party of the United States and was turned down. In October 1877 he founded his own organization and gave it a sentence to shout.

SOURCE TEXT

The Chinese must go.
Denis Kearney, Workingmen's Party of California, from October 1877

Within two years his party held a third of the seats at California's constitutional convention.

The core idea +

Four of these demands were the ones students have spent three lessons watching workers fight and lose. The fifth was granted. Laid out in one column, the pattern is not subtle, and it is the argument of the entire hour.

Read the full story +

Set five records from these lessons beside one another: what workers asked for and what happened in the cases shown. This is a comparison of selected struggles, not a claim that labor never won anything else. The Knights’ 1885 railroad victory is one counterexample.

The eight hour day was demanded nationally on a set date in May 1886 by somewhere between three hundred thousand and five hundred thousand workers. Congress did not set a general standard workweek for private industry until the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, fifty two years later, and even then it worked by making longer hours expensive rather than by forbidding them.

Wage restoration was the demand at Martinsburg in 1877, at Homestead in 1892, and at Pullman in 1894. In each case the cut stood.

Union recognition was the demand at Homestead. The Amalgamated Association was destroyed there, and union power in the steel industry was severely weakened.

The right to refuse to handle a company's cars was tested at Pullman in 1894 and answered with a federal injunction on July 2 and a Supreme Court ruling on May 27, 1895.

Exclusion of Chinese labor was demanded by the Workingmen's Party of California from 1877, adopted by both national parties in their 1880 platforms, and signed into law on May 6, 1882.

04MARCH 3, 1875

THE FIRST LAW

Seven years before the Chinese Exclusion Act, Congress passed a law barring the entry of women brought for immoral purposes.

Inspectors could not easily prove why a woman was traveling. They could easily tell she was a woman.

The core idea +

The Page Act is the first restrictive federal immigration law in American history, and on paper it is about trafficking. In practice it was the instrument that kept Chinese women out. A population that cannot form families does not replace itself, which is why the law that came seven years later did not need to be permanent in order to be permanent.

Read the full story +

The Page Act took effect on March 3, 1875. It did three things. It made it a crime for an American citizen to bring a person from Asia into the country for forced labor. It barred anyone convicted of a crime who had not finished a sentence. And in Section 3 it forbade bringing women into the United States for the purposes of prostitution.

The first two provisions required an official to establish something about a contract or a criminal record on the far side of the Pacific. The third required an official to look at a person and form an opinion. Consular officers in Hong Kong were given authority to decide whether a woman was traveling for what the statute called lewd and immoral purposes, and a woman who could not satisfy them did not board the ship.

The effect shows up in the census within five years. In the early 1870s there were roughly seventy eight Chinese women in the United States for every thousand Chinese men. By 1880 the figure was about forty seven per thousand.

Chinese men in America were already living apart from wives and children by economics. After 1875 that separation was policy. The community that grew in American cities through the 1880s was overwhelmingly male, aging, and severely restricted in its ability to form families and raise a second generation of citizens by birth.

House: 202 to 37.

Senate: 32 to 15.

President Chester A. Arthur signed it.

The law suspended Chinese laborers from entering the country for ten years and barred every Chinese immigrant already here from ever becoming a citizen.

HOUSE OF REPRESENTATIVES202—37
UNITED STATES SENATE32—15
The core idea +

This was a major federal law explicitly barring Chinese laborers as a group. It was not close, it was not fringe, and it was not a southern or western oddity. It passed the House by better than five to one and the Senate by better than two to one and was signed by a Republican president from New York.

Read the full story +

Arthur had already vetoed one version. In April 1882 Congress sent him a bill suspending Chinese immigration for twenty years, and he returned it, arguing it violated the Angell Treaty the United States had signed with China two years earlier, which permitted the United States to limit but not to prohibit Chinese immigration. Congress rewrote the term from twenty years to ten and sent it back. He signed it on May 6.

The act suspended the immigration of Chinese laborers, skilled and unskilled, for ten years. It required Chinese residents who wanted to leave and return to obtain certificates. And it stated that no state or federal court could admit a Chinese person to citizenship. Merchants, students, teachers, diplomats, and travelers were exempt on paper, and the burden of proving membership in one of those categories fell on the person claiming it.

The argument for the bill had been made most memorably three years earlier by Senator James G. Blaine of Maine, a former Speaker of the House and a future Secretary of State, in language the American Federation of Labor was still reprinting in 1902.

SOURCE TEXT

You cannot work a man who must have beef and bread alongside of a man who can live on rice.
The result is not to bring up the man who lives on rice to the beef and bread standard, but to bring down the beef and bread man to the rice standard.
Senator James G. Blaine, 1879, quoted approvingly in Meat vs. Rice, American Federation of Labor, 1902

The clearest voice against it belonged to Senator George Frisbie Hoar of Massachusetts, an abolitionist who had spent his career on civil rights and who had argued in Congress for the eight hour day and for the right to strike.

SOURCE TEXT

Nothing less than the legalization of racial discrimination.
Senator George Frisbie Hoar of Massachusetts, on the Chinese Exclusion Act

He lost.

The core idea +

Between 1890 and 1920 the Chinese population of the United States fell by nearly half while the population of the country grew sharply. The law did not merely stop growth. It reversed it, and it kept reversing it for thirty years.

Read the full story +

The census counted 34,933 Chinese people in the United States in 1860 and 105,465 in 1880. Over those twenty years the community had tripled. It peaked at 107,488 in 1890, eight years after exclusion, and then began falling. It was 89,863 in 1900, 71,531 in 1910, and 61,639 in 1920.

Nothing about that decline was mysterious. Men who left could not come back. Men who stayed grew old in a population with almost no women and almost no children. Men who died were not replaced. The community shrank by subtraction, one funeral at a time, as the population declined.

The population did not pass its 1890 level again until after the Second World War.

07SEPTEMBER 2, 1885

ROCK SPRINGS

The Union Pacific coal mines in Wyoming Territory employed 331 Chinese miners and about 150 white miners.

The white miners were organized. The Chinese miners were paid less.

By that evening 28 Chinese miners were dead, 15 were wounded, and 79 homes had burned.

Sixteen men were arrested. A grand jury declined to indict any of them.

28KILLED · AT LEAST
79HOMES BURNED
0INDICTMENTS
The core idea +

Exclusion did not calm the West. It ratified the position that Chinese workers did not belong, and the three years after 1882 were the most violent in the entire history of Chinese settlement in America. Rock Springs was one of the deadliest attacks, and its aftermath showed how the people responsible could escape prosecution.

Read the full story +

The Union Pacific Coal Department worked its Wyoming mines with two labor forces. Chinese miners, 331 of them by 1885, worked for less. White miners, most of them Cornish, Irish, Swedish, and Welsh, had formed a Knights of Labor local in 1883 and had watched the company use the wage difference against them for years.

On the morning of September 2 a dispute over which crew had the right to work a particular room in Number Six mine turned into a beating. By afternoon armed white miners had gathered, given the Chinese camp an hour to leave, and then opened fire on it. They burned it as people ran. At least twenty eight Chinese miners were killed, fifteen were wounded, and seventy nine houses were destroyed. Survivors walked toward Evanston, about a hundred miles west, and some were picked up along the tracks by trains.

Federal troops of the Seventh Infantry arrived on September 5. On September 9 six companies escorted the surviving Chinese miners back to Rock Springs, where they were put back to work under military guard. Federal protection did not bring the attackers to justice.

Sixteen men were arrested. A Sweetwater County grand jury heard the case and returned no indictments. Its statement to the court is the primary source for this section.

SOURCE TEXT

We have diligently inquired into the occurrence at Rock Springs ... though we have examined a large number of witnesses, no one has been able to testify to a single criminal act committed by any known white person that day.
Sweetwater County grand jury, Wyoming Territory, 1885

The arrested men were released on October 7. Congress later appropriated $147,748.74 and paid it to the Chinese government. The payment was for property. It was not compensation for the dead, and the United States attached a statement that it was made as a matter of generosity rather than obligation.

08NOVEMBER 3, 1885

THE METHOD

Tacoma's mayor gave the city's Chinese residents until November 1 to leave.

On November 3 a crowd of several hundred, including the police and the fire chief, went house to house and told people to pack.

They were marched eight miles through rain to a rail station outside town.

Twenty seven men were later indicted. None were prosecuted.

EXPULSIONS & VIOLENCE / SELECTED RECORDS08

The log

Jean Pfaelzer’s research documents at least 200 expulsions of Chinese communities in California alone between 1849 and 1906.

The core idea +

Rock Springs was a massacre. Tacoma was a procedure, run by elected officials, with a published deadline and an orderly march. It was copied across the West because it worked and because nobody involved was punished. Killing was not the common method. Paperwork and a deadline were.

Read the full story +

Jacob Weisbach, a German immigrant who had become mayor of Tacoma, called a mass meeting at the Alpha Opera House on February 21, 1885. About nine hundred people attended.

SOURCE TEXT

If the people are in earnest, if they are Free Americans in fact, they will not yield up their homes and business to the filthy horde.
Mayor Jacob Weisbach, Tacoma, February 21, 1885

On September 28 he presided over an Anti Chinese Congress in Seattle that set a regional deadline of November 1. By the time the deadline arrived, roughly seven hundred of the estimated one thousand Chinese residents in Pierce County had already gone.

On November 3 a crowd that started around two or three hundred and grew toward five hundred moved through the remaining Chinese homes and businesses. It included prominent merchants, city police, and the fire chief. Between one hundred fifty and two hundred people were ordered out, and at half past one in the afternoon they were marched eight miles in heavy rain to the Lake View rail station, where they were made to buy tickets to Portland or ride in boxcars. Two men died of exposure waiting for transportation.

Twenty seven men, Weisbach among them, were indicted on federal conspiracy charges. The indictments were dismissed on technical grounds. New indictments were brought and dismissed the same way. Nobody stood trial.

Newspapers across the West began calling it the Tacoma Method. Seattle attempted the same thing in February 1886 and the territorial governor declared martial law to stop it.

The core idea +

The government issued a document, then cancelled the document, then refused to honor it, and the Supreme Court held unanimously that it was entitled to do all three. The case became a foundation of the broad congressional authority known as plenary power.

Read the full story +

Chae Chan Ping was a laborer who had lived in San Francisco for twelve years. Under the 1884 amendments to the exclusion act, a Chinese laborer who wished to visit China could obtain a certificate entitling him to return. He obtained one and sailed on the steamer Belgic on June 2, 1887.

The Scott Act was signed on October 1, 1888. It declared all such certificates void and of no effect. Roughly twenty thousand Chinese laborers who were outside the United States holding valid return certificates found that the papers in their pockets had become worthless while they were away. Chae Chan Ping's steamer docked in San Francisco on October 7. He was detained on board and refused permission to land.

His case reached the Supreme Court, which decided it on May 13, 1889 under the name Chae Chan Ping v. United States, and it is still commonly called the Chinese Exclusion Case. The Court ruled against him unanimously. Justice Stephen Field wrote that the power to exclude foreigners belongs to the government as an attribute of sovereignty, that Congress may exercise it whenever it judges the public interest requires, and that the decision is conclusive upon the courts.

That holding has a name. It is the plenary power doctrine, and it gave Congress exceptionally broad authority over immigration, with courts generally deferring to exclusion decisions. It did not eliminate every form of judicial review. It was announced in a case about one man and a cancelled piece of paper, and it became a lasting influence on American immigration law.

The core idea +

The Geary Act made a document into a condition of remaining in the country. Its burden of proof fell on Chinese residents. For a laborer seeking a court exception after missing registration for an unavoidable reason, Section 6 required a credible white witness to establish prior residence.

Read the full story +

Representative Thomas J. Geary of California introduced the bill on February 18, 1892. President Benjamin Harrison signed it on May 5.

The act extended exclusion for ten more years and required Chinese laborers entitled to remain in the United States to apply for certificates of residence within a year. Other Chinese residents could obtain certificates voluntarily. Section 7 specified identifying information; a photograph requirement was added in November 1893.

Section 3 placed the burden of proof on the person arrested under the law. Section 4 prescribed up to a year of imprisonment at hard labor followed by removal for those adjudged not entitled to remain. In 1896, Wong Wing v. United States held that punitive imprisonment required the constitutional protections of a criminal trial.

Section 6 ordered deportation for a laborer found without a certificate after the deadline unless a judge accepted accident, sickness, or another unavoidable reason for not obtaining it. Prior residence then had to be established by at least one credible white witness. The racial test shown in this file belongs to that court exception, not to every ordinary certificate application.

Roughly one hundred ten thousand people were affected by the registration campaign.

The core idea +

The record of this period runs in two directions at once. Everything so far has been done to Chinese immigrants. From here the hour follows what they did, and what they did was sue. Chinese residents brought thousands of cases in American courts during exclusion, and some of what they won protects everyone in the country now.

Read the full story +

Lee Yick arrived in San Francisco in 1861 and ran a laundry in a wooden building for twenty two years. In 1880 the city passed Order Number 156, which required a permit from the Board of Supervisors to operate a laundry in a wooden building. Laundries in brick or stone buildings needed no permit.

The ordinance said nothing about race. Roughly three hundred twenty laundries in the city were in wooden buildings, and more than two hundred of them were Chinese owned. The city systematically refused Chinese applicants while approving nearly all applicants who were not Chinese.

Lee Yick kept operating, was arrested, refused to pay the fine, and took the case up. The Supreme Court decided it on May 10, 1886, and ruled for him without a dissent. Justice Stanley Matthews wrote the opinion.

SOURCE TEXT

Though the law itself be fair on its face and impartial in appearance, yet, if it is applied and administered by public authority with an evil eye and an unequal hand, so as practically to make unjust and illegal discriminations between persons in similar circumstances, the denial of equal justice is still within the prohibition of the Constitution.
Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, decided May 10, 1886

The Court also held that the Fourteenth Amendment's guarantee of equal protection applies to all persons within the territorial jurisdiction of the United States, not only to citizens. Lee Yick was not a citizen and could not become one. He won anyway.

5—3THE COURT UPHELD THE LAW
MAY 15, 1893
The core idea +

This was one of the largest organized acts of civil disobedience in nineteenth century America, and almost nobody has heard of it. It was funded, coordinated, and carried out by people who were barred from citizenship, and it was a genuine gamble that failed in court.

Read the full story +

The Chinese Consolidated Benevolent Association, known as the Six Companies, was the umbrella organization of Chinese district associations in San Francisco. When the Geary Act passed in May 1892, the Six Companies issued instructions across the country. Do not register. Contribute to a legal fund instead. The plan was to force a test case with the entire population as the plaintiff, and to have the law struck down before the registration year expired.

The great majority complied with the boycott rather than the statute. Of roughly one hundred ten thousand people subject to the act, only a small fraction had registered when the year ran out in May 1893. Those without the required certificates now faced arrest and deportation under the act.

The test case was Fong Yue Ting v. United States. The Supreme Court decided it on May 15, 1893, ten days after the registration deadline. The Court upheld the Geary Act by five votes to three. Justice Horace Gray wrote that the power to expel foreigners rests on the same footing as the power to exclude them, and upheld the deportation provisions against the constitutional challenge.

Justice Stephen Field dissented, and so did Justice David Brewer and Chief Justice Melville Fuller. Field, who four years earlier had written the opinion against Chae Chan Ping, drew a line at expelling people already lawfully resident without a trial.

The government did not have the money or the officers to deport a hundred thousand people, and the mass removal never happened. Congress passed an amendment in November 1893 extending the registration period by six months. This time, with the constitutional argument gone, people registered.

The core idea +

The exclusion laws produced the case that settled birthright citizenship for the whole country. A cook kept on a boat for five months won a landmark interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment’s citizenship guarantee.

Read the full story +

Wong Kim Ark was born at 751 Sacramento Street in San Francisco around 1873, to Chinese parents who ran a merchant business there and who could not naturalize under the 1882 act. He worked as a cook. He visited China and returned in 1890 and was readmitted as a native born citizen without trouble.

He went again and returned in August 1895 aboard the steamer Coptic. This time the collector of customs at San Francisco refused to let him land, on the theory that a child born in the United States to Chinese subjects was himself a Chinese subject and therefore excluded. He was held aboard ships in the harbor for five months while the case went forward.

The Supreme Court decided United States v. Wong Kim Ark on March 28, 1898, and ruled in his favor six votes to two. The Fourteenth Amendment says that all persons born or naturalized in the United States, and subject to the jurisdiction thereof, are citizens. The Court held that this means what it says, read against the common law rule the country had inherited, and that a child born on American soil to parents who are themselves permanently barred from citizenship is nonetheless a citizen at birth.

Chief Justice Melville Fuller dissented, joined by Justice John Marshall Harlan.

The core idea +

The law was not an event. It was a condition, and it lasted longer than most of the students' grandparents have been alive. The final instrument does not argue. It runs the years next to things students already know happened in them, and the same line repeats beside every one.

Read the full story +

The immigration station on Angel Island opened on January 21, 1910 and operated until 1940. The Angel Island Immigration Station Foundation estimates that about 100,000 Chinese people were detained there, and unlike arrivals at Ellis Island, who were usually processed in hours, Chinese arrivals were detained in locked barracks while a Board of Special Inquiry examined them. Inspectors asked questions designed so that only a person from the claimed family and village could answer. How many steps to the door of your house. Which direction does your village face. Who lived in the third house on the row. Answers were checked against relatives interviewed separately, and a contradiction could mean removal.

People waited weeks and months. Some waited longer. They wrote on the walls, and then carved what they had written into the wood, and the station painted over it and they carved again. More than two hundred of those poems have been recovered.

Carved into the barracks wall at Angel Island, author unknown

A fire destroyed the administration building in 1940 and the station closed.

Exclusion ended on December 17, 1943, in the middle of a war in which China was an American ally. The Magnuson Act repealed the exclusion laws and made Chinese immigrants eligible for naturalization. It set the number of Chinese people who could enter the United States each year at one hundred five.

01nativism+

The political position that people born in a country should be favored over immigrants, and that immigrants threaten the nation

Kearney's party in San Francisco, October 1877

02exclusion+

Barring a specific group from entering a country by law

WHERE YOU MET IT
May 6, 1882

03naturalization+

The legal process by which an immigrant becomes a citizen

WHERE YOU MET IT
The 1882 act closed it to Chinese immigrants entirely

04indemnity+

A payment for loss or damage. The United States denied legal obligation in the Rock Springs payment.

WHERE YOU MET IT
The $147,748.74 Congress paid China for the houses burned at Rock Springs

05certificate of residence+

An identity document Chinese laborers were required to obtain under the Geary Act to establish their right to remain.

WHERE YOU MET IT
The Geary Act, May 5, 1892

06burden of proof+

Which side has to prove the case. In criminal law it is normally the government

WHERE YOU MET IT
Section 3 put it on the Chinese person instead

07plenary power+

Exceptionally broad governmental authority; in immigration, courts have generally deferred to Congress, though judicial review is not entirely absent.

WHERE YOU MET IT
Chae Chan Ping, decided May 13, 1889

08equal protection+

The Fourteenth Amendment guarantee that the law treats people in the same situation the same way

WHERE YOU MET IT
Lee Yick's laundry permit, May 10, 1886

09birthright citizenship+

Citizenship acquired at birth under the Fourteenth Amendment’s rule for persons born in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction.

WHERE YOU MET IT
Wong Kim Ark, decided March 28, 1898

10civil disobedience+

Breaking a law deliberately and publicly in order to challenge it

WHERE YOU MET IT
The refusal to register under the Geary Act in 1892

How to read this hub

The railroad, mining camp, and steamship scenes are AI-authored illustrations. They are not archival photographs. Document interfaces are teaching reconstructions. The statute scan is an authentic National Archives document.

Primary records, court opinions, and institutional histories support the narrative. The comparative growth line is arithmetic, not a historical prediction or a causal estimate. The outcome column compares five selected records.

Historical racist statements appear as attributed evidence. No invented portraits or decorative Chinese writing are used.
