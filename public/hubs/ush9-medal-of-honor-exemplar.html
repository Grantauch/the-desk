U.S. History 9 · Medal of Honor project

Freddie Stowers

He kept them moving

A report by Sample Partner A and Sample Partner B
Partner A: poster lead · Partner B: screen lead

Teacher-created exemplar · fictional authors

Step 1 · Both partners

Fact file

Born · A
Freddie Stowers was born on January 12, 1896, in Sandy Springs, South Carolina. [1]
Before the war · A
He worked on his family’s farm. He and his wife, Pearl, had a daughter named Minnie Lee. [1]
Joining up · A
The Army drafted him in 1917, when he was 21 years old. [1]
Rank and unit · B
He was a corporal and squad leader in Company C, 371st Infantry Regiment, 93rd Division. [2]
The action · B
His Medal of Honor action happened on September 28, 1918, at Hill 188 in France. [2]
Medal and death · B
He died on September 28, 1918. His sisters accepted his Medal of Honor on April 24, 1991. [1]

Why this works

Every question gets a specific answer. Dates, places and the unit can be checked. The writer keeps the battle date separate from the award date.

Try it: Could someone find your veteran using only your fact file?

Step 2 · Three words each

Hard words, explained simply

These six words appear in Stowers’s official citation. [2]

Each partner explains three different citation words
PartnerWordMy explanation
AceasedStopped doing something. Here, the Germans stopped firing.
AparapetsRaised edges of a trench that help protect the soldiers inside.
AinterlockingOverlapping each other. The gunfire crossed, making it harder to find a safe gap.
BcasualtiesPeople killed or wounded in this fight. It does not mean everyone died.
BmortallyIn a way that causes death. A mortal wound is one the person does not survive.
BintrepidityBravery when facing danger. Here, it describes Stowers continuing the attack.

Why this works

The explanations sound like someone talking to a friend. Several connect the word to this battle, showing that the writer understands the citation.

Check yours: A definition should make the original sentence easier to understand.

Step 3 · Partner A

Before the war

Freddie Stowers grew up in Sandy Springs, South Carolina. He had a wife named Pearl and a daughter named Minnie Lee. [1]

Before the Army, Stowers worked on the family farm. He already had a family and a job before he became a soldier. [1]

He was drafted into the Army in 1917. His unit was made up of Black soldiers, and it later served with French forces. [1] His story also shows that the American Army was segregated.

Why this works

The writer introduces a person with a life before the battle. Each short paragraph answers one of the three workspace questions.

The last sentence connects his experience to segregation. There is no invented conversation or claim about what he secretly thought.

Step 3 · Partner B

The day he earned the medal

On September 28, 1918, Stowers was a squad leader attacking Hill 188 in France. His company was leading the attack. [2]

German soldiers acted like they were surrendering. When the Americans came into the open, the Germans started shooting again. More than half the company became casualties. [2]

First, Stowers led men toward a machine-gun position, which they destroyed. Next, he pushed toward a second trench. He was wounded but kept urging his men forward until he died. The company continued and helped capture the hill. [2]

Why this works

Place and job → danger → actions → result. That order makes the battle understandable.

The writer explains what Stowers actually did. Calling someone “brave” becomes convincing when you show the action.

Step 4 · Partner A

The citation in plain English

These three paragraphs follow the three citation parts shown in the project workspace. [2]

The first part identifies Stowers and explains the false surrender. The Americans moved into the open and were caught by gunfire.

The second part describes him leading the attack against a machine gun. His squad destroyed the position, and he kept the attack going.

The last part says he continued encouraging his squad after being fatally wounded. His example helped the men continue. The citation honors his leadership and sacrifice.

Why this works

The meaning and order stay accurate, but the wording and sentence structure change. That is paraphrasing.

Try it: Read one citation part, look away, and explain what happened out loud before you write.

Step 4 · Partner B

After the war

Stowers did not come home after the fight. He died that day and is buried at the Meuse-Argonne American Cemetery in France. [3]

His Medal of Honor recommendation was not processed. After the Army investigated, President George H. W. Bush awarded the medal in 1991. His two sisters accepted it for the family. [1]

In 1991, Stowers became the first Black American honored with the Medal of Honor for World War I service. [3] A statue at Anderson University also remembers him. [4]

Why this works

“After the war” still has an answer when a veteran died in combat. This section follows his burial, recognition and remembrance.

The delayed award is documented. The writer does not invent a particular person’s motive for the lost recommendation.

Step 5 · Written separately

Why he matters

Partner A · What surprised me?

The part that surprised me most was how long his family waited for the medal. He died in 1918, but the award came in 1991. [1] I expected an award for something that serious to come much sooner. It made me wonder whose stories still have not been recognized.

Partner A · Courage or America in 1918?

His story shows me that serving your country did not mean your country treated you equally. He served in a Black unit, and his recommendation went unprocessed for decades. [1] I think remembering his courage also means paying attention to how recognition can fail people.

Partner B · What surprised me?

I was most surprised that he kept encouraging the squad after he was badly wounded. [2] I cannot know what he felt, but his actions show that he was still focused on the men around him. That detail made the story feel more personal to me.

Partner B · Courage or America in 1918?

To me, courage here means taking responsibility when people need someone to lead. The citation says his men continued after he died. [2] His leadership mattered because it affected what other people did, even when he could no longer go with them.

Why this works

Each partner has a different response. Each answers both questions: a specific moment and an explanation of what it means.

“I think” and “to me” identify interpretation. The evidence is what makes the reflection more than “he was a hero.”

Try it: After naming your detail, add “This matters to me because…” and explain.

Step 6 · Partner A

The wall poster

My scene and layout plan

I will draw Stowers leading other soldiers up the hill toward two trench lines, so the main picture shows his leadership.

I put a short headline at the top, the drawing in the middle, four facts underneath and a line from the citation at the bottom. I used simple shapes and colored-pencil colors because I want the story to be easy to follow.

In your project, mark the poster Done after you finish and hand in the actual paper poster.

Why this works

The four-word headline fits the five-word limit. Four facts, the veteran’s name, one clear scene and a ten-word citation quote make the poster complete.

The drawing shows an action supported by the source. Simple art can communicate a strong idea.

Check yours: Can a person across the room tell who it honors and what he did?

Step 6 · Partner B

The memorial screen

My title

He kept them moving

My caption

This shows Stowers leading his squad toward the trenches on Hill 188.

My citation quote

“he inspired his men to follow him in the attack” [2]

Why I designed it this way

I used the pencil to draw the slope and three helmets to stand for the squad. The poppy is a remembrance symbol. I left space around the name and quote so people can read them on the classroom board.

Why this works

The title and caption point to the same moment. The quote is copied accurately, stays under twelve words, and has a source.

The drawing is achievable with the tools in Step 6. The symbols have a purpose, and the name is easy to read.

Sources I used

I started with Stowers’s veteran page. The numbers in my report point to these records.

  1. National Park Service, “Freddie Stowers.” Family, draft, service and delayed recognition.
  2. U.S. Army, World War I Medal of Honor citations, “Stowers, Freddie.” His official citation, battle actions and vocabulary.
  3. American Battle Monuments Commission, Black History Month commemoration. Burial and recognition.
  4. U.S. Army, “Centennial Profile: Cpl. Freddie Stowers.” The Anderson University statue and his service.

Why this works

A reader can check the facts. The report points to government historical records and identifies the official citation.

Sources support the history. The personal reactions and design explanations belong to the model writers.

Step 7 · Both partners

Our final check

  • Six fact answers and six different citation words, split equally.
  • All four chapters, with an answer for every writing box.
  • Two personal reflection answers from each partner.
  • Poster: short headline, scene, four facts and a short citation quote.
  • Memorial screen: short title, caption, quote and finished design.
  • Names, dates, spelling and the full paper checked against our sources.

We would each open Finish, check our own jobs, preview the combined paper and press our own Turn In button. The paper poster also has to be handed in.

Use this on your project

Being complete is the starting point. Strong work also explains the evidence clearly, connects the reflection to a real detail, and makes deliberate art choices.
