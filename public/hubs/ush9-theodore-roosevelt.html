01 New York City · Childhood

THE BOY WHO COULD NOT BREATHE

“I was a sickly, delicate boy”Theodore Roosevelt · 1913

Theodore Roosevelt had severe asthma. Some nights, he sat up gasping while his parents tried to help him. He grew up in a wealthy family, with support many sick children did not have. He later described feeling nervous, timid, and physically helpless.

02 Adolescence · Training

HE TRAINED HIMSELF TO BE STRONG

“I decided that I would try to supply its place by training.”Theodore Roosevelt · 1913

After two boys bullied him, Roosevelt decided to learn boxing. He wanted to be able to defend himself. Improvement took years.

His own account suggests that fear of helplessness helped drive his training. Later, he kept choosing hard physical challenges. That connection is an interpretation of his words.

03 Dakota Territory · 1880s

HE WENT WEST

Roosevelt went to the Dakota Badlands to hunt and ranch. Outdoor work gave him another way to test himself.

His ranch business lost money, but the experience helped shape his public image and his concern for wildlife. The wealthy New Yorker wanted to prove he could handle a demanding life.

04 United States · 1898

WHO WERE THE ROUGH RIDERS?

When the United States went to war with Spain, Roosevelt helped organize the 1st U.S. Volunteer Cavalry. The Rough Riders were a regiment, a military unit within a larger force.

Cowboys and college athletes served alongside Native American volunteers. Leonard Wood first commanded them. Roosevelt became their commander after Wood was promoted.

05 Tampa to Cuba · 1898

CAVALRY WITHOUT ITS HORSES

Cavalry usually fought or traveled on horseback. Transport ships had limited space, so most of the Rough Riders' horses stayed in Florida. The men fought in Cuba on foot.

Some officers brought horses. Roosevelt rode his horse Texas during part of the famous charge, then dismounted.

06 San Juan Heights, Cuba · July 1, 1898

THE CHARGE AND ITS COST

San Juan Heights · Schematic, not to scale.
A victory involving several Army units.

Roosevelt's best-known charge first went up Kettle Hill, part of the San Juan Heights. The Rough Riders fought alongside regular Army units, including Black soldiers of the 9th and 10th Cavalry. The victory belonged to a larger force.

07 Washington, D.C. · 1901-1909

THE PRESIDENT

War helped make Roosevelt a national celebrity. In 1901, President William McKinley was assassinated, and Vice President Roosevelt took office. Roosevelt was 42.

He used presidential power to challenge a major railroad trust and regulate food and drugs. His drive now shaped decisions affecting millions of people.

08 Mississippi · November 1902

THE TEDDY BEAR STORY

On a hunting trip led by Holt Collier, a Black guide who had been born enslaved, Roosevelt refused to shoot a bear that had been captured and tied up. He considered that unsporting. A cartoon turned the episode into a famous image. Morris and Rose Michtom made a toy called Teddy's Bear.

The bear in the hunt was an adult, not the cute cub of the popular story. Roosevelt also said the injured animal should be killed to end its suffering.

09 Amazon basin, Brazil · 1914

THE RIVER OF DOUBT

Years after leaving the White House, Roosevelt joined a scientific expedition in Brazil with his son Kermit. Brazilian explorer Candido Rondon co-led the expedition. Their team would map the River of Doubt, a river in the Amazon basin.

It was poorly mapped by outsiders, in a region inhabited by Indigenous peoples. Rapids damaged boats. Moving supplies around them took exhausting work.

10 River of Doubt · April 1914

TOO SICK TO KEEP GOING

Roosevelt hurt his leg while helping with a canoe. The injury became infected, and he suffered fever. He could barely walk.

The expedition nearly killed him. He survived with the help of the doctor, Kermit, Rondon, and the rest of the team. Personal determination had reached its limit.

“I could hardly hobble, and was pretty well laid up.”Theodore Roosevelt · 1914

11 Return to his presidency · 1901-1909

WHAT HE PROTECTED

230 MILLION ACRES

Public land protected during his presidency

During his presidency, Roosevelt helped protect about 230 million acres of public land. His administration added five national parks and protected forests, wildlife reserves, and national monuments. Different protections served different purposes: national forests could allow managed use of resources.

In 1908, he protected the Grand Canyon as a national monument. It became a national park in 1919. National parks already existed before Roosevelt, and the National Park Service was created in 1916, under Woodrow Wilson.

These places were also Indigenous homelands. Conservation belongs in a history that includes the people who already lived there.

“Leave it as it is. You cannot improve on it.”Theodore Roosevelt · 1903
