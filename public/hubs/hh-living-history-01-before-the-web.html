Information existed. But getting it took time, transportation, help from other people, and sometimes money. Meanwhile, universities and research labs already had connected computers. The future existed—just not equally for everyone.

CHAPTER 02THE DOORWAY

FIRST,
YOU HAD
TO DIAL.

For many home users in the 1990s, getting online meant using a dial-up modem. It turned computer data into signals that could travel over an ordinary telephone line. While it connected, you heard a strange mechanical conversation. On many home setups, using the line for a voice call and connecting to the internet at the same time wasn't possible.

The world didn't arrive in your pocket. You waited for it to connect.

CHAPTER 03HOW A NETWORK BECAME A WORLD

THE INTERNET
IS NOT THE WEB.

This is the detail most people miss. The internet is the network of networks that moves information. The Web is one of the things built on top of it. Email existed before websites. You could be online without opening a web page.

THE INFRASTRUCTURE

A NETWORK
OF NETWORKS.

Different institutions built local networks. Shared technical rules—especially TCP/IP—let their machines exchange data across separate networks. No single person or company owns the whole thing.

A 1983 transition helped make TCP/IP a common language for network communication.

OCTOBER 29, 1969 · CALIFORNIA

TWO LETTERS,
ONE NEW IDEA.

Researchers at UCLA and the Stanford Research Institute attempted to send LOGIN across ARPANET. The first transmission stalled after “LO.” They soon fixed it. A new way for distant computers to communicate was taking shape.

WHY IT MATTERSThis wasn't a home internet service. The project had support from a U.S. defense research agency, and the people at the keyboard were researchers and programmers. Later generations would build on their work.
CHAPTER 04AN IDEA LEAVES THE LAB

THEN IT
REACHED
YOUR STREET.

It wasn't a single invention. It was a handoff.

Publicly funded research helped create the network. Tim Berners-Lee at CERN proposed the Web to connect scientific documents. CERN made the Web software freely available in 1993. Browser makers made pages easier to navigate. Internet service providers brought access into homes, and businesses began experimenting with what customers could do online.

By 1995, the U.S. research backbone NSFNET had given way to commercial internet networks. A network originally associated with research was becoming a place for shopping, meeting people, publishing ideas, and making money.

NOT THE WORK OF ONE PERSON
Here's the surprise: the Web grew partly because its creators didn't charge everyone a fee to use its basic design.’93

CHAPTER 06THE BILL FOR BEING CONNECTED

THE WORLD
OPENED UP.
SO DID THE RISKS.

The internet gave people extraordinary power to learn, publish, organize, sell, and communicate. It also made new kinds of harm possible. The history doesn't stop at the invention.

+

MORE VOICES.

A teenager could publish a page; a small store could reach distant buyers; faraway relatives could talk more often. You didn't always need a newspaper, television station, or major retailer to reach people.

−

MORE GATEKEEPERS.

As the Web grew, search engines, platforms, and internet providers became influential. Algorithms and companies could shape what people saw and which businesses got noticed.

↗

FASTER ANSWERS.

Information could spread worldwide in seconds. Students gained access to archives, lessons, and communities that might never have existed locally.

!

FASTER FALSEHOODS.

Rumors, scams, and deceptive content could travel just as quickly. Privacy also became harder as browsing and online activity created data about ordinary people.

THE COUNTERFACTUAL LABHISTORY THAT DIDN'T HAPPEN

DELETE
THE WEB.
NOW WHAT?

Imagine it's still 2026, but the World Wide Web never becomes the mass public system we know. Other technologies might still exist. What changes for your family, school, and town? Pick one. This is a thought experiment, not a prediction.

ACTUAL 2026

A library that never closes.

Search an article, open an online archive, watch a lecture, message a classmate. Finding information is easier; checking it is still your responsibility.

ALTERNATE 2026 · PLAUSIBLE, NOT CERTAIN

Information still has a closing time.

Books, television, local networks, and other digital systems might still develop. But without a widely shared public Web, finding and publishing information could remain more fragmented and more tied to institutions.
