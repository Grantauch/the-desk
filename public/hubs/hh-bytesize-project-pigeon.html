byte 01

A very small pilot

During World War II, researchers were trying to improve how weapons reached moving targets. Psychologist B. F. Skinner proposed an unusual guidance system: trained pigeons inside a nose cone. His work used a basic learning principle. A behavior followed by a reward may become more likely. The bird did not need to understand war, geography or a flight plan. It needed to perform a trained response to a visible image. S1, S2

byte 02

Peck the picture

The experimental system presented a target image to a pigeon. Training encouraged pecks at the target, and those responses could be used in a guidance concept. A surviving Smithsonian nose cone has compartments for three birds. This is not a story about pigeons carrying messages or homing to a loft. Those are different uses of the same species. Here the proposed contribution was visual tracking inside an experimental apparatus. S1

byte 03

Reward is not understanding

Use the invented training board. Choosing the target produces a reward; choosing the background does not. This is a conceptual demonstration, not an animal-learning experiment or a historical performance record. The point is that a reliable-looking behavior can be shaped without the subject understanding the whole system. You can distinguish “the bird pecked the image” from “the bird understood the mission.”

byte 04

The cereal company enters the story

General Mills provided technical support, and the project received wartime government attention. The work was more substantial than an amusing sketch on a napkin. Later, the Navy revived the idea under the name Orcon, short for organic control. But a funded program and a convincing demonstration still leave questions about reliability, integration and the alternatives available to a military service. S3

The task and the mission

A trained response

A bird can learn to respond to a target image. That can be observed directly.

An operational claim

A fielded weapon requires the complete system to work reliably in conditions a laboratory demonstration may not reproduce.

byte 05

Watch what the camera really shows

The Skinner Foundation preserves a silent film showing target training. Look for the response the film actually records. A bird pecking at a display establishes that behavior under those conditions. It does not show a missile reaching an enemy ship. A film can be authentic while an exciting caption asks it to prove more than it contains. byte 06

The demonstration did not become a mission

The birds were never used in combat, and the later program ended as electronic guidance became a more practical option. The remaining apparatus helps document a serious, strange engineering approach. It does not establish that pigeon-guided weapons fought a secret war. A useful ending gives the research its due while keeping the laboratory, the proposed system and operational service separate.
