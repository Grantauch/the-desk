The Gilded Age, 1862 to 1914
Rails/Oil/Steel/The Law of 1882/Detroit

In 1873 Mark Twain and his neighbor Charles Dudley Warner published a novel called The Gilded Age. Something gilded is not solid gold. It is cheap metal with a coat of gold paint so thin you could scratch it off with a fingernail.

The name stuck to the years after the Civil War. The United States laid track across a continent, pumped oil, poured steel and built the richest fortunes the world had ever seen. Every one of those fortunes was built by somebody's hands, and most of those hands did not end up rich.

The Railroad1862 to 1869

On July 1, 1862, in the middle of the Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln signed the Pacific Railway Act. Two companies would build a railroad to connect the country. The Central Pacific started in Sacramento, California, and built east. The Union Pacific started in Omaha, Nebraska, and built west.

The government paid them with land and loans. For every mile of track, a company got 10 square miles of public land, and Congress doubled that to 20 in 1864. It also lent them $16,000 a mile on flat ground, $32,000 in the high plains and $48,000 in the mountains.

Who laid the track

The Central Pacific had the Sierra Nevada mountains in front of it and almost nobody willing to work there. In 1865 it hired its first crews of Chinese immigrants. By February 1867 more than 11,000 Chinese men worked for the company, about 90 out of every 100 workers it had.

In June 1867 about 3,000 Chinese workers put down their tools. They asked for $40 a month and a shorter workday. Charles Crocker, the boss in charge of construction, cut off their food and supplies at the camps high in the mountains. The strike ended in about a week. The workers went back without a raise.

On April 28, 1869, a Central Pacific crew of Chinese workers, with eight Irish rail handlers, laid 10 miles and 56 feet of track in one day. It is still the record for track laid by hand in a single day.

May 10, 1869

The two railroads met at Promontory Summit, Utah. Leland Stanford, president of the Central Pacific, swung a silver hammer at a golden spike and, by most accounts, missed. A telegraph operator sent one word to the whole country: DONE. A trip from New York to San Francisco that took around six months by wagon or ship now took about a week.

Look at the photo at the top of this page. It was taken that day and it became the most famous picture of the railroad. Chinese workers had laid the last rails that morning, but almost none of them appear in it.

One hundred years later, at the 1969 anniversary ceremony, the U.S. Secretary of Transportation, John Volpe, gave a speech praising the builders. Descendants of Chinese railroad workers were in the audience. He said:

Who else but Americans could have laid ten miles of track in 12 hours?

John Volpe, Promontory Summit, May 10, 1969

The Oil1870 to 1911 · Cleveland, Ohio

Cleveland was full of oil refineries in the early 1870s, and they were all fighting each other on price. Rockefeller made a secret deal with the railroads. Standard Oil got a cheaper shipping rate than everybody else. Then he went to his competitors one at a time with an offer to buy them out. Owners who said no found out they could not compete with his prices.

Horizontal integrationBuying up your competitors, the companies that do the same thing you do, until you control the whole market.

The day of combination is here to stay. Individualism has gone, never to return.

John D. Rockefeller, 1880 · quoted in Allan Nevins, John D. Rockefeller

Ida Tarbell spent years digging through court records and company papers. Starting in 1902 she published her findings in McClure's magazine, then as a book in 1904, The History of the Standard Oil Company. On May 15, 1911, the Supreme Court ruled that Standard Oil had broken the Sherman Antitrust Act and ordered it split into 34 separate companies. Rockefeller owned shares in all of them and got richer after the breakup.

The Steel1848 to 1901 · Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania

Carnegie opened his Edgar Thomson steel works outside Pittsburgh in 1875. Railroads needed steel rails, and cities needed steel beams for bridges and tall buildings. Rockefeller bought out his competitors. Carnegie did something different. He bought every step it took to make steel, so he never had to pay another company a profit along the way.

Vertical integrationOwning every stage of making your product, from the raw materials in the ground to the finished goods.

Carnegie also wrote about what rich men owed everyone else. In 1889 he published an essay that became known as "The Gospel of Wealth." A rich man, he wrote, should give his fortune away to build things that help ordinary people improve themselves.

The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced.

Andrew Carnegie, "Wealth," North American Review, June 1889

Homestead, 1892

At the Homestead steel works, men worked 12 hour shifts. Every two weeks, when crews switched between days and nights, they worked a 24 hour shift called the long turn. In 1892 Frick cut pay and locked the union out of the mill. Carnegie was at his castle in Scotland.

Before dawn on July 6, 1892, about 300 armed guards from the Pinkerton Detective Agency came up the Monongahela River on two barges. Thousands of steelworkers and their families were waiting on the riverbank. The shooting lasted most of the day. Seven workers and three Pinkerton guards were killed before the guards surrendered.

Six days later, 8,000 Pennsylvania militia soldiers took over the town. The mill reopened with replacement workers, and the union at Homestead was finished. In 1901 Carnegie sold his company to the banker J. P. Morgan for $480 million. Morgan used it to build U.S. Steel. Carnegie spent the rest of his life giving away about $350 million, including money for 2,509 public libraries.

The Law of 1882The Chinese Exclusion Act
Read this whole source carefully. There is one question at the end.

Go back to Source A. More than 11,000 Chinese men built the Central Pacific. The railroad was finished in May 1869, and almost all of them lost their jobs that same month. Many moved to San Francisco and other western towns and looked for any work they could find, in laundries, farms, fisheries and factories.

In 1873 the country fell into a long depression. Jobs disappeared. In California, many white workers blamed the Chinese, who often worked for less pay. In 1877 a San Francisco speaker named Denis Kearney drew huge crowds with a slogan he repeated at every rally: "The Chinese must go!"

The 1880 census counted 105,465 Chinese people in the entire United States. That was about 2 out of every 1,000 Americans. Congress passed a law anyway, and on May 6, 1882, President Chester A. Arthur signed it. This was the first time the United States had ever banned people from coming here because of their nationality.

What the law said

Here is the actual wording of three parts of the law. Open each one to read what it means.

Section 1"...the coming of Chinese laborers to the United States be, and the same is hereby, suspended..."
What it means

Chinese workers could no longer come to the United States. The ban was supposed to last ten years.

It did not end after ten years. Congress renewed it in 1892 and made it permanent in 1902.

Section 14"...no State court or court of the United States shall admit Chinese to citizenship..."
What it means

Chinese immigrants who were already here could never become American citizens, no matter how long they lived here.

Without citizenship they could not vote, which meant they had no way to vote out the politicians who passed the law.

Section 15"...the words 'Chinese laborers'...shall be construed to mean both skilled and unskilled laborers and Chinese employed in mining."
What it means

Almost every kind of Chinese worker counted as a laborer. The only groups still allowed in were small ones, like merchants, teachers, students, diplomats and travelers.

Proof on paper

In 1892 the Geary Act went further. Every Chinese laborer living in the United States had to register with the government and carry a certificate of residence with his photograph on it. A man caught without one could be arrested and deported.

The Car1863 to 1914 · Dearborn and Detroit, Michigan

Henry Ford was born on July 30, 1863, on a farm in Springwells Township, which is now part of Dearborn. He hated farm work and loved taking watches apart. In 1879, at 16, he walked to Detroit and got a job as an apprentice machinist.

By 1893 he was chief engineer at the Edison Illuminating Company in Detroit, keeping the city's electric plant running. At night he worked on an engine in a brick shed behind his house on Bagley Avenue. On June 4, 1896, his first car was finished. He called it the Quadricycle. It ran on four bicycle wheels, had two speeds and no reverse, and the shed door was too narrow to get it out. Ford took an axe to the brick wall and drove it through the hole.

Ford started the Ford Motor Company in 1903. In 1908 he came out with the Model T. His goal was a car ordinary working people could afford. On December 1, 1913, his Highland Park plant started running a moving assembly line. The car moved down the line and each worker did one small job over and over. Building the chassis of a Model T dropped from about 12 and a half hours to about 93 minutes.

The work was so boring and so fast that men quit constantly. In 1913 Ford had to hire more than 52,000 people to keep about 14,000 jobs filled. On January 5, 1914, he announced a new wage of $5 a day, about double the old pay. Thousands of men lined up outside the plant the next morning. The $5 came with strings. Investigators from Ford's Sociological Department visited workers' homes to check how they lived, drank and spent their money before they got the full amount.

Ford also used Carnegie's idea. At his River Rouge plant in Dearborn, built starting in 1917, Ford owned the iron mines in Michigan's Upper Peninsula, the ships that carried the ore across the Great Lakes, the steel mill, the glass plant and the railroad. Iron ore came in one end of the Rouge and finished cars went out the other.
