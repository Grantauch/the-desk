The battlefield changed.

Machines could kill farther, faster, and from safer positions. The people who carried them still had to cross the same muddy ground. Explore the equipment and questions behind two interactive WWI galleries—even if your device cannot draw 3D.

About the images and models: The 3D objects in the full galleries are interpretive reconstructions, not photographs or exact engineering measurements. This guide is a readable alternative, not a replacement for studying primary sources.

Gallery 01 · Ten objects

The Arsenal

Look beyond the weapon's shape. Ask what problem it was designed to solve—and what new problem it created for the people facing it.

01 / ARMOR

Mark I tank · 1916

Built to cross broken ground and trench lines, early tanks offered protection but were slow, mechanically unreliable and difficult to coordinate.

Investigate: Why might a weapon that can cross a trench still fail to change a whole battle?
02 / INFANTRY

SMLE Mk III rifle

British infantry carried bolt-action rifles with a trained soldier controlling the pace of aimed fire. They fought alongside machine guns and artillery, not instead of them.

Investigate: What could a rifle accomplish that heavy artillery could not?
03 / DEFENSE

Vickers machine gun

Water cooling helped sustained fire from protected positions. A relatively small crew could cover open ground that attackers had to cross.

Investigate: Which side benefited most when an attack moved across open fields?
04 / ARTILLERY

QF 18-pounder

Field guns delivered shrapnel and high-explosive fire. Bombardments damaged roads and ground, but did not always destroy deep shelters or wire.

Investigate: Why did more shells not guarantee a clear path for infantry?
05 / AIR

Sopwith Camel · 1917

Fighters contested the skies as airplanes also served observers gathering information about enemy positions and movements.

Investigate: Why might reliable information matter as much as winning an aerial duel?
06 / PROTECTION

Small-box respirator

Filter respirators improved soldiers' protection against certain chemical attacks. Protection depended on conditions, training and keeping the equipment ready.

Investigate: How does a new defense change the effectiveness of an earlier weapon?
07 / OBSTACLES

Barbed wire and screw pickets

Wire slowed movement, exposed attackers and strengthened defenses. Artillery sometimes left it intact, turning a planned advance into a trap.

Investigate: Why was wire especially dangerous when paired with machine guns?
08 / ASSAULT

Wex flamethrower · 1917

A specialized short-range weapon used against defended positions. Its dramatic appearance could not overcome the limits of range, fuel and exposure.

Investigate: What practical difficulties might outweigh the surprise of a new weapon?
09 / THE HOME FRONT

Zeppelin airship

Airship raids extended the war above the front lines, bringing violence and fear to some civilian communities far from the trenches.

Investigate: How did attacks on civilians change what people understood a battlefield to be?
10 / AT SEA

German U-boat

Submarines attacked shipping, affecting supplies and diplomacy. Germany's unrestricted submarine warfare became a major factor in the United States entering the war.

Investigate: How could an action at sea influence a country's decision to join the war?

Gallery 02 · Questions from the trench

Over the Top

The full 3D experience lets you walk a reconstructed American trench in 1918. Use this alternative field assignment to investigate how a trench worked and what soldiers faced there.

FIELD QUESTION 01

Supplies

A front-line position needed food, clean water, ammunition, equipment and routes to carry wounded people back.

Think: Which supply failure would become dangerous fastest, and why?
FIELD QUESTION 02

Observation

A periscope or lookout position helped soldiers see without exposing their heads above the parapet.

Think: What can an observer learn—and what remains invisible from ground level?
FIELD QUESTION 03

Protection

Sandbags, helmets and dugouts reduced different dangers. None made a trench entirely safe.

Think: Why did soldiers need several kinds of protection at once?
FIELD QUESTION 04

Chemical warfare

Respirators changed what soldiers could survive, but maintaining a seal and responding quickly were crucial.

Think: How could fear and uncertainty affect a unit even when protective gear worked?
FIELD QUESTION 05

No man's land

Between lines lay cratered ground, obstacles and exposed routes. Terrain could stop attackers before they reached enemy defenses.

Think: Which obstacles could be spotted on a map, and which required first-hand observation?
FIELD QUESTION 06

American troops

By 1918 U.S. forces were serving in major offensives while learning to fight a war already transformed by years of attrition.

Think: What might newly arriving troops need to learn from the armies already fighting?
FIELD QUESTION 07

The evidence

A reconstructed scene helps you picture a place, but photographs, maps and soldiers' accounts can confirm or challenge its details.

Think: What claim about trench life would you want a primary source to verify?
