US History 9 · World War I · Reading edition

The battlefield changed.

In 1914 most people expected the war to be over by Christmas. It lasted more than four years and killed about 9 million soldiers. This is the reading edition of two 3D galleries, with every placard, number and question from the full versions.

About the 3D galleries: The models there are interpretive reconstructions, simplified and not always to scale. The facts on these pages are the same ones the galleries use.

Gallery 01 · Ten weapons

The Arsenal

These ten weapons are a big reason the war lasted more than four years.

I · 1914

The Rifle

Every army marched to war in 1914 believing the soldier with a rifle would decide it.

Aimed shots a minute
15, for a well trained British soldier
Effective range
about 600 yards
British rifle
Short Magazine Lee-Enfield
American rifle
M1903 Springfield

Before the war, the British Army drilled its men for the Mad Minute. A soldier had sixty seconds to put 15 aimed shots into a target 300 yards away, working the bolt and reloading as he went. Stories from the Battle of Mons in August 1914 claimed German officers believed they were facing machine guns when it was really trained riflemen.

Rifles never went away. Every soldier in this hall still carried one. What changed was everything around the rifleman. The weapons in the rest of this gallery could kill him from much farther away, and in much larger numbers, than any man with a bolt action could answer.

Question: A good rifleman could fire 15 aimed shots a minute. Keep that number in your head as you walk the hall. Which later exhibit do you think made that number look small, and why?
II · 1884 / 1914

The Machine Gun

An American inventor living in London built the gun that stopped attacks dead on the Western Front.

Rounds a minute
about 450 to 600
Crew
usually six men
German MG 08 with sled mount
about 140 pounds
Cooling
a jacket of water around the barrel

Hiram Maxim, an American living in Britain, built the first fully automatic machine gun in 1884. The kick of each shot loaded the next round. As long as the canvas belt kept feeding, the gun kept firing. The British Vickers and the German MG 08 that faced each other in 1914 were both built on his design.

Firing that fast heated the barrel until the water in the jacket boiled. Vickers crews piped the steam into a can so they could pour the water back in. Two or three guns placed to sweep open ground could stop an attack made by hundreds of men, and that is exactly how the armies used them.

Question: A trained rifleman fired 15 aimed shots a minute. A machine gun fired about 450 to 600 rounds. Using those numbers, explain what happens to a line of soldiers walking across open ground toward a single machine gun.
III · 1914 to 1918

The Big Guns

Soldiers feared the shells more than anything else on the battlefield.

Wounds caused by shells
most estimates near 6 in 10
Shells fired before the Somme
about 1.5 million in 7 days
French 75 field gun
up to 15 rounds a minute
German Paris Gun, 1918
hit Paris from about 75 miles away

Artillery fired from miles behind the front. The gunners almost never saw the men they killed, and the men they killed almost never saw the guns. A bursting shell threw jagged steel in every direction, and a near miss could bury a man alive in his own trench.

Before the Battle of the Somme in 1916, British guns fired about 1.5 million shells over seven days. Commanders expected the barrage to wreck the German trenches and shred the barbed wire. German soldiers rode out the storm in dugouts as deep as 30 feet. When the guns stopped on the morning of July 1, they climbed back up to their machine guns.

What passing-bells for these who die as cattle?
Only the monstrous anger of the guns.

Wilfred Owen, British officer and poet, from "Anthem for Doomed Youth," 1917
Question: Most estimates say shells caused about 6 in every 10 wounds. If shells did that much damage, why would generals keep ordering infantry attacks instead of only firing artillery?
IV · 1915

Poison Gas

Scientists in German labs turned a factory chemical into a weapon that drifted on the wind.

First large attack
April 22, 1915, near Ypres, Belgium
Chlorine released that day
about 168 tons from 5,730 cylinders
Gas casualties, all armies
about 1.3 million
Gas deaths
roughly 90,000

Late on the afternoon of April 22, 1915, German engineers opened thousands of steel cylinders along four miles of trench near Ypres. The wind carried a yellow green cloud across to French and Algerian colonial troops. Chlorine attacked their lungs as they breathed it in, and a hole about four miles wide opened in the Allied line.

Within months both sides were using gas. Every time masks improved, chemists developed a more dangerous gas. Mustard gas, used from July 1917, blistered skin and blinded eyes, and it soaked into the mud where it could hurt men days after the shells landed.

Gas! GAS! Quick, boys!—An ecstasy of fumbling,
Fitting the clumsy helmets just in time

Wilfred Owen, from "Dulce et Decorum Est," written 1917
Question: Gas caused about 1.3 million casualties but roughly 90,000 deaths, far fewer than the shells killed. Use details from this placard to explain why soldiers dreaded it anyway.
V · 1874 / 1915

Barbed Wire

A fence invented for cattle ranchers on the American plains.

Patented
1874, by Joseph Glidden of DeKalb, Illinois
Original use
fencing cattle on the open range
German wire at the Somme
belts as much as 30 yards deep
Silent tool
screw pickets twisted into the ground at night

Barbed wire spread across the American West because it was cheap and it kept cattle where ranchers wanted them. Forty years later the same idea stretched across France and Belgium in thick tangled belts. Wiring parties crawled into no man’s land after dark to fix it, twisting corkscrew posts into the ground so no hammer would give them away.

Wire did not kill many men by itself. Its job was to hold attackers in place, and it always sat right where the machine guns were aimed. A soldier caught on the barbs could not move forward and could not get back.

Question: Barbed wire was an American farm invention. Explain how a tool built for ranching turned deadly when it was paired with a machine gun.
VI · 1915

The Flamethrower

The most terrifying weapon in the hall had the shortest reach.

German name
Flammenwerfer
First used against the British
July 30, 1915, at Hooge, Belgium
Range
about 20 to 40 yards
Crew
two men for the backpack version

German assault engineers carried tanks of oil and pressurized gas on their backs. One man carried the tank and a second man aimed the nozzle, spraying a stream of burning oil into an enemy trench. At Hooge, near Ypres, British soldiers woke before dawn on July 30, 1915, to jets of fire pouring over their parapet.

The weapon could clear a trench in seconds, but its crews had to get within a few dozen yards to use it. Soldiers on both sides aimed for flamethrower teams first. Compare its reach with the other weapons in the lab and you can see why.

Question: A flamethrower reached about 20 to 40 yards. A rifle was effective out to about 600 yards. What does that gap tell you about how dangerous it was to carry one?
VII · 1916

The Tank

Britain built it in secret to get across no man’s land. The first ones broke down constantly.

First battle
September 15, 1916, Flers-Courcelette, France
Weight
about 28 tons
Crew
8 men
Top speed
under 4 miles per hour, about a fast walk

British designers hid their project behind a cover story. Factory workers were told they were building water carriers, and the code word tank stuck. The machine was meant to flatten barbed wire and cross trenches while machine gun bullets bounced off its armor, so infantry could follow behind it.

Inside, eight men shared the space with a roaring engine. Engine heat and exhaust fumes made crews sick, and bullets hitting the outside knocked hot flakes of steel off the inside walls. Early tanks broke down constantly. A year later at Cambrai, in November 1917, more than 400 British tanks attacked together and punched a hole several miles deep into the German line.

VIII · 1914 to 1918

The Airplane

Eleven years after the Wright brothers, airplanes went to war.

First job
scouting enemy movements
1915 breakthrough
a gear that fired bullets between propeller blades
The Red Baron
80 victories, killed April 21, 1918
Top American ace
Eddie Rickenbacker, 26 victories

In 1914 airplanes were for watching. Pilots flew over enemy lines, marked troop movements on maps and dropped weighted message streamers to commanders below. During the Battle of the Marne, French scouts spotted a gap opening between two German armies, and the Allies attacked into it.

In 1915 the Dutch designer Anthony Fokker gave German planes an interrupter gear that timed each shot to pass between the spinning propeller blades. A pilot could now aim his whole airplane at an enemy and fire. Most Allied pilots flew without parachutes for the entire war.

Question: Airplanes started the war as scouts. How could knowing where the enemy was be just as valuable as shooting at him?
IX · 1915

The Zeppelin

Bombs fell on British homes hundreds of miles from the trenches.

First raid on Britain
January 19, 1915
Lifting gas
hydrogen, which burns
Length of later airships
about 650 feet, almost two football fields
People killed by Zeppelin raids in Britain
557

German airships crossed the North Sea at night and dropped bombs on British towns and on London. Most raids did little military damage, but they shocked a country that had never been attacked from the sky. British towns began dimming their lights at night so the airship crews could not find them.

Searchlights and fighter pilots hunted the airships. Pilots learned that special bullets could set the hydrogen on fire. On the night of September 3, 1916, Lieutenant William Leefe Robinson shot down a German airship over Cuffley, north of London, and crowds across the city watched it fall in flames.

Question: Zeppelin raids killed relatively few people. Why might they still have mattered to how the British public felt about the war?
X · 1915 / 1917

The U-Boat

The weapon that pulled the United States into the war.

Lusitania sunk
May 7, 1915, off the coast of Ireland
Died
1,198 people, 128 of them American
Unrestricted submarine warfare resumed
February 1, 1917
United States declares war
April 6, 1917

The British Navy blockaded Germany to cut off its food and supplies. Germany answered with submarines, called U-boats, that sank ships headed for Britain. On May 7, 1915, a single torpedo from U-20 hit the British liner Lusitania. She sank in about 18 minutes.

American anger forced Germany to promise it would stop attacking passenger ships without warning. In February 1917 Germany broke that promise and resumed unrestricted submarine warfare, gambling it could starve Britain before America arrived. Combined with the Zimmermann Telegram, it brought the United States into the war that April.

Question: The Lusitania sank in May 1915, but the United States did not declare war until April 1917. Using this placard, explain what finally changed in 1917.

Stop 1 · 1917 to 1918

Over There

You are standing in a front line trench in France. The men who stood here in 1918 were Americans, most of them younger than twenty five and a long way from home.

The United States declared war on April 6, 1917, with an army of only about 128,000 men. Congress passed the Selective Service Act that May. About 24 million men registered for the draft, and about 2.8 million were drafted. By the armistice nearly 2 million Americans had crossed the Atlantic. The French and British called them doughboys.

General John J. Pershing, commander of the American Expeditionary Forces, refused to hand his men over to fill gaps in Allied units. He wanted them to fight as an American army under American officers. Most Americans did not see heavy combat until the spring of 1918.

Stop 2 · Every day

Living in the Ditch

Most days in the trenches nobody attacked anybody. The enemy was the cold, the water and everything that lived in the mud.

Men stood to with rifles ready at dawn and at dusk, the times attacks were most likely. The rest of the day was sentry duty, digging, carrying and trying to sleep. Rats were everywhere in the trenches. Lice lived in every seam of every uniform. American soldiers called them cooties.

Standing in cold water for days caused trench foot. Feet swelled, went numb and could turn gangrenous, and some men lost toes or whole feet. Officers inspected feet daily and ordered men to change into dry socks. Units rotated through the front line, then support trenches, then rest areas behind the lines, so no one stayed at the very front for weeks at a time.

stand to at dawn and dusk
Biggest daily enemies

Stop 3 · 1918

The Harlem Hellfighters

The 369th Infantry Regiment spent more days under fire than any other American unit in the war, and they did it wearing French helmets.

The 369th was an African American regiment from New York. The U.S. Army, segregated by race, would not let Black soldiers fight beside white American troops, so it loaned the 369th to the French Army. The French gave them French helmets and rifles and sent them to the front. The regiment served about 191 days in combat.

On the night of May 15, 1918, Private Henry Johnson and Private Needham Roberts were on sentry duty when a German raiding party of about twenty men attacked. Both Americans were badly wounded. Johnson kept fighting with grenades, the butt of his rifle and finally a bolo knife until the Germans fled. He took 21 wounds. France awarded him the Croix de Guerre. The United States did not award him the Medal of Honor until 2015, 86 years after his death.

There wasn’t anything so fine about it. Just fought for my life. A rabbit would have done that.

Henry Johnson, 369th Infantry, after returning home

Stop 4 · No man’s land

Do Not Look Up

A soldier who raised his head over the parapet in daylight could be dead before he knew a sniper had seen him.

The ground between the two front lines was called no man’s land. In some places the enemy trench was less than a hundred yards away. In others it was half a mile. It was a stretch of overlapping shell holes and broken wire, with bodies lying where no one could safely reach them.

To watch it safely, men used trench periscopes, two mirrors in a wooden box that let a soldier see over the top while his head stayed below it. At night, patrols crawled out to repair wire, listen at enemy lines or raid a trench to grab prisoners. Flares drifted down on small parachutes and lit the ground bright white. Anyone caught standing in the open held perfectly still until the flare burned out, because movement drew fire.

Distance between lines
under 100 yards to about half a mile
Safest way to look
a trench periscope
Night work
patrols, wiring parties and raids

Stop 5 · October 2 to 8, 1918

The Lost Battalion

Cut off in a ravine in the Argonne Forest, about 550 Americans held out for six days with almost no food and very little water.

Major Charles Whittlesey’s men pushed forward as ordered on October 2, but the units on their flanks did not. German troops closed in behind them. For days the trapped Americans fought off attacks from every side and drank from a stream the Germans had covered with machine guns.

Then American artillery, aiming at the Germans, began landing shells on the trapped battalion itself. Whittlesey sent his last carrier pigeon, Cher Ami. The bird was shot through the breast, lost most of a leg and was blinded in one eye, and still reached the American lines about 25 miles away. The shelling stopped. When the battalion was finally relieved, only about 194 men walked out.

We are along the road parallel 276.4. Our own artillery is dropping a barrage directly on us. For heaven’s sake stop it.

Message carried by Cher Ami, October 4, 1918

Stop 6 · October 8, 1918

Sergeant York

A Tennessee farmer who first asked not to fight became the most famous American soldier of the war.

Alvin York grew up in the mountains of Tennessee and belonged to a church that taught against violence. When he was drafted he asked to be excused as a conscientious objector. He was turned down. After long talks with his battalion commander, he decided he could fight with a clear conscience.

On October 8, 1918, in the Argonne, York was one of 17 soldiers sent behind German lines to silence machine guns. After nine of them were killed or wounded, York, a crack shot from years of hunting, took on the machine gunners. By the end of the fight he and the seven survivors marched 132 German prisoners back to the American lines. He received the Medal of Honor, and in 1941 Hollywood made a movie of his life.

Stop 7 · September 26 to November 11, 1918

Over the Top

This ladder is the last thing between you and no man’s land.

The Meuse-Argonne offensive put more than a million American soldiers into one battle, the largest in American history. For 47 days they attacked through forests and ridges the Germans had spent four years fortifying. It cost 26,277 American lives.

War was not the only killer. In 1918 a deadly influenza swept the world, and crowded troop ships and camps spread it fast. Of the 116,516 Americans who died in military service during the war, 53,402 were killed in battle. More than 63,000 died of other causes, most of them from disease, led by influenza and pneumonia.

Americans in the offensive
more than 1 million
Americans killed
26,277 in 47 days
All U.S. military deaths

Question: More than half of American military deaths in World War I did not come from combat. Why would living conditions at the front and on troop ships make that possible?

11 a.m., November 11, 1918

German officials signed the armistice in a railroad car in the forest of Compiègne at about 5 a.m. It would take effect six hours later, at the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month.

Fighting went on right up to the last minute. Private Henry Gunther of Baltimore charged a German machine gun at 10:59 a.m. and is usually counted as the last American soldier killed in the war. Then the guns stopped.

American sound ranging equipment recorded the sound of enemy guns so they could be located. A strip of its film from the morning of November 11 shows the moment the war ended, as the lines go flat.
