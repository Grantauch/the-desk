What the presenter says

My heel is Fritz Von Erich. His real name was Jack Adkisson, and he was not German at all. He was born in 1929 in Jewett, Texas, and he played college football at SMU. Around 1953 he started wrestling as Fritz Von Erich, a German giant billed from Munich. His finishing hold was the Iron Claw, where he grabbed an opponent's head and squeezed.

To understand why that worked, you have to remember the timing. World War II ended in 1945. About 405,000 Americans died fighting it, and a lot of the men in the crowd in the 1950s had fought Germany in person. Eight years later, here was a wrestler on their TV playing a Nazi.

Questions 3 and 4

How Wrestling Used the War

The name
Adkisson took his mother's maiden name, Erich, and added "Von" so it sounded like German nobility.
The partner
A fake brother named Waldo Von Erich, played by Walter Sieber, the Canadian son of a German immigrant. Waldo goose stepped around the ring.
The act
Fritz gave the Nazi salute before his matches and finished opponents with the Iron Claw.
Why the crowd hated himFor people who had lived through the war, a Nazi was the clearest villain they knew. Every time an American wrestler beat Fritz, the crowd got to see Germany lose again.
Primary source I make a much better heel than babyface.

Jack Adkisson, in a September 1953 letter to Dallas promoter Ed McLemore. In the same letter he explained that he had been wrestling in New England as a German giant billed from Munich. Jack Pfefer Wrestling Collection, University of Notre Dame.

Q3 · 15 of 15Three specific details: the invented name, the fake brother, the salute and the hold.

Q4 · 15 of 15The boos are tied to 1953, to people who lived through the war, not to wrestling in general.

Extra strengthA primary source in his own words proves the German act was a business choice.

What the presenter says

Promoters built every part of this character around the war. The name came from his mother's maiden name, Erich, with Von added to sound like German nobility. They gave him a fake brother, Waldo Von Erich, who was really Walter Sieber, the Canadian son of a German immigrant. Fritz gave the Nazi salute, and Waldo goose stepped.

My favorite source is a letter Adkisson wrote to a Dallas promoter in September 1953. In it he says, I make a much better heel than babyface. That letter is in the Jack Pfefer collection at Notre Dame, so we know the German act was a choice he made for money.

Crowds hated him because a Nazi was the one villain everybody agreed on. When an American beat Fritz, the whole arena got to watch Germany lose one more time.

What Fritz Tells Us

Question 5

What Fritz Tells Us About America

Fritz with four of his sons, the heroes of Texas wrestling in the 1980s. Wikimedia Commons.

My answerTwenty years after the war, American crowds still saw Germany as the enemy, and promoters made money off that anger. The act was unfair to ordinary Germans. It ended once Adkisson needed his own hometown to cheer for him.
The twistIn 1966 Adkisson bought the Dallas wrestling office and made himself a good guy. His sons became the biggest heroes in Texas, a story told in the 2023 film The Iron Claw.

Q5 · 15 of 15Names the prejudice, takes a side on fairness and backs it with the 1966 switch.

Sources · 10 of 10Two credible sources, and neither one comes from WWE.

What the presenter says

Here is the twist. In 1966 Adkisson bought the Dallas wrestling office and turned himself into a good guy. His sons became the biggest heroes in Texas, and their story is the 2023 movie The Iron Claw. The man did not change. The Nazi act stopped making sense once the war was twenty years old and he needed Dallas to cheer for him.

So what does Fritz tell us about America? Two decades after the war, crowds still saw Germans as the enemy, and promoters made money off that anger. I think the act was unfair to ordinary Germans, but it worked because the war had made Nazis the villain everybody understood. My sources are the Notre Dame special collections article about his 1953 letter and a 2023 article in The Ringer, and neither one comes from WWE.
