The Tapes That Ended a Presidency
Installed 1971·Revealed 1973·Released 1974

For a year, the case against the President came down to one man's memory.

In June 1973 John Dean, the former White House lawyer, swore to a Senate committee that President Richard Nixon had helped cover up the Watergate break in. The White House said Dean was lying to save himself, and nobody could check. Three weeks later a quiet former aide answered one question, and the case changed. The President had been recording himself.

This investigation follows the tapes from a secret system in the White House to the Supreme Court and asks what made the case provable.

Part One · The WordSources A and B
A
The AccusationJune 25, 1973
REEL 01

On June 17, 1972, five men were arrested inside the Democratic Party's offices at the Watergate building in Washington. The money in their pockets traced back to the committee working to reelect President Nixon. That November Nixon won 49 states.

John Dean had been counsel to the President until Nixon fired him on April 30, 1973. On June 25 he spent the day reading a 245 page statement to the Senate Watergate Committee on live television. He said he had warned Nixon in the Oval Office on March 21, 1973.

I began by telling the President that there was a cancer growing on the Presidency and that if the cancer was not removed that the President himself would be killed by it.

John Dean, testimony to the Senate Watergate Committee, June 25, 1973

Dean had helped run the cover up himself, and the White House said he was blaming his boss to get a lighter sentence. Senator Howard Baker of Tennessee, the committee's top Republican, kept asking one question: what did the President know, and when did he know it?

Put these four kinds of evidence in order, strongest first. Use the arrows.

    One defensible ranking, and why:

    1. A recording made the day it happened. It does not depend on anyone's memory or honesty, though it still needs a known date and known voices.
    2. Sworn testimony from someone in the room. Firsthand, and lying under oath is a crime. Memory still drifts.
    3. An anonymous source feeding tips to reporters. Useful for knowing where to dig, but nobody can question a person with no name.
    4. The accused person's public denial. It comes from the person with the most to lose. The bottom two are close.

    B
    The QuestionJuly 13 and 16, 1973
    REEL 02

    On Friday, July 13, Senate staff interviewed Alexander Butterfield, a former aide who had worked a few steps from the Oval Office. A Republican staff lawyer, Donald Sanders, asked whether Dean might be right that some conversations had been recorded. Butterfield told him the truth. Three days later, on live television, Fred Thompson asked him again.

    THOMPSON: Mr. Butterfield, are you aware of the installation of any listening devices in the Oval Office of the President?

    BUTTERFIELD: I was aware of listening devices, yes, sir.

    Senate Watergate Committee, July 16, 1973

    In February 1971 the Secret Service had hidden seven microphones in the Oval Office and two in the Cabinet Room, and later added four in Nixon's private office across the street. The Oval Office microphones switched on by themselves when someone spoke. Only a handful of people knew, including Nixon, his chief of staff H. R. Haldeman, and Butterfield. By July 1973 the system had captured about 3,400 hours of conversation.

    Within days, the committee and the special prosecutor, Archibald Cox, demanded the tapes. Nixon refused. He said a president's private conversations were protected by executive privilege, the idea that presidents need to get honest advice without every word becoming public.

    B2Before July 16 it was Dean's word against Nixon's. What could investigators do after July 16 that they could not do before?

    C
    The GapOctober 1973 to January 1974
    REEL 03

    Cox took the President to court and won. Instead of handing over the tapes, Nixon ordered Attorney General Elliot Richardson to fire Cox on Saturday, October 20, 1973. Richardson resigned rather than do it. His deputy, William Ruckelshaus, refused as well and left. The third man in line, Solicitor General Robert Bork, fired Cox. The newspapers called it the Saturday Night Massacre.

    Whether ours shall continue to be a government of laws and not of men is now for Congress and ultimately the American people.

    Archibald Cox, statement after his firing, October 20, 1973

    Within days of the public outcry Nixon agreed to give up the tapes. On November 17 he told a room of newspaper editors, People have got to know whether or not their President is a crook. Well, I'm not a crook.

    Four days later his lawyers told Judge John Sirica that one tape had a problem. On a June 20, 1972 conversation between Nixon and Haldeman, three days after the break in, 18 and a half minutes of voices had been replaced by a loud buzz. Haldeman's own handwritten notes from that meeting show that Watergate was one of the topics.

    Nixon's secretary, Rose Mary Woods, testified that she may have erased about five minutes by accident. She said she reached for a ringing phone while her foot was still on the recorder's pedal.

    The 18 and a Half MinutesJune 20, 1972 · Nixon and Haldeman
    0:005:0010:0015:0018:30

    In January 1974 a panel of six audio experts, chosen jointly by the court and the White House, examined the tape and the machine. No one has ever recovered what was said.

    D
    The RulingApril to July 1974
    REEL 04

    The new special prosecutor, Leon Jaworski, subpoenaed 64 more conversations. On April 29, 1974, Nixon went on television beside a stack of binders and released more than 1,200 pages of edited transcripts instead of the tapes. Wherever the President had cursed, the pages said expletive deleted.

    The case reached the Supreme Court. On July 24, 1974, the justices ruled 8 to 0 that Nixon had to hand the tapes over. Justice William Rehnquist sat out because he had worked in Nixon's Justice Department. Chief Justice Warren Burger, whom Nixon had appointed, wrote the opinion.

    The generalized assertion of privilege must yield to the demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a pending criminal trial.

    United States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683, July 24, 1974

    The Court did not throw out executive privilege. It set a limit on it. Three days later the House Judiciary Committee began voting on articles of impeachment, the formal charges that could send a president to trial in the Senate.

    Part Three · The TapeSource E
    E
    The Smoking GunRecorded June 23, 1972 · released August 5, 1974
    REEL 05

    One of the conversations the Court forced into the open was recorded on June 23, 1972, six days after the break in. Haldeman told Nixon the FBI was tracing the burglars' money. His plan was to have the deputy director of the CIA, Vernon Walters, tell the acting head of the FBI, L. Patrick Gray, to back off by claiming the case touched national security. It did not.

    HALDEMAN: . . . have Walters call Pat Gray and just say, "Stay the hell out of this" . . .

    NIXON: Right, fine.

    NIXON: Play it tough. That's the way they play it and that's the way we are going to play it.

    Oval Office, June 23, 1972 · Watergate trial exhibit transcript, Nixon Library

    For two years Nixon had said he did not learn about the cover up until Dean told him in March 1973. This tape showed him approving it six days after the arrests. It was released on August 5, 1974. Charles Wiggins of California, the President's strongest defender on the Judiciary Committee, announced that he would now vote to impeach. Other Republicans who had voted no followed him.

    On August 7 three Republican leaders, Senator Barry Goldwater, Senator Hugh Scott and House leader John Rhodes, went to the White House. Accounts of that meeting differ, but the number of senators Goldwater said were still with Nixon was somewhere between about 15 and 18. Removing a president takes 67 votes in the Senate, so Nixon needed 34 to survive.

    The Senate Count100 seats · August 7, 1974
    Goldwater's lower count, 15Up to 18 in other accountsNeeded to survive, 34

    Even the highest count left him at least 16 votes short.

    On the night of August 8 Nixon told the country he would resign. At 11:35 the next morning his letter reached Secretary of State Henry Kissinger.

    I hereby resign the Office of President of the United States.

    Richard Nixon, letter to Henry Kissinger, August 9, 1974

    A month later, on September 8, 1974, President Gerald Ford granted Nixon a full pardon.
