    The Great War · 1914 to 1920

    Over There

    For almost three years Americans watched the deadliest war in history from across an ocean. Then they sent two million soldiers to France. Follow the war from a street corner in Sarajevo to the Senate floor in Washington.

    The big question

    “The world must be made safe for democracy.”

    President Woodrow Wilson, asking Congress to declare war on Germany, April 2, 1917

    Sarajevo · June 28, 1914

    Two shots on a street corner

    Gavrilo Princip, a 19 year old Bosnian Serb, shoots Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary, and his wife Sophie. Princip belongs to a group that wants Bosnia free of Austrian rule.

    Belgium and France · August to September 1914

    The war digs in

    Germany storms through neutral Belgium toward Paris. French and British troops stop it at the Marne River in September. Both armies dig trenches, and by winter the lines run all the way from the North Sea to Switzerland.

    Off the coast of Ireland · May 7, 1915

    A liner goes down

    A German submarine, U-20, fires one torpedo into the British ocean liner Lusitania. The ship sinks in 18 minutes, eleven miles from the Irish coast. Americans are furious, but the United States stays out.

    A secret offer

    Germany’s foreign minister, Arthur Zimmermann, sends a coded telegram to Mexico. If the U.S. enters the war, Germany will help Mexico win back Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. British codebreakers in London read every word.

    Chapter I · June 28 to August 4, 1914

    Thirty-Seven Days

    Europe’s great powers had spent years building armies and signing alliances. One assassination set the whole system moving, one declaration at a time.

    Why it spread

    A chain, not a spark

    The assassination did not have to start a world war. It did because every big power had promised to fight for someone else. Russia had promised to protect Serbia. Germany had promised to back Austria-Hungary. France was tied to Russia. Britain had sworn to defend Belgium’s neutrality in a treaty from 1839.

    The generals made it worse. Germany’s war plan, the Schlieffen Plan, said that if Russia mobilized its army, Germany had to knock out France first by marching through Belgium. Once Russia called up its soldiers on July 30, the plan left Germany almost no time to talk.

    The German chancellor called the 1839 treaty “a scrap of paper.” Britain declared war anyway on August 4.

    Chapter II · 1914 to April 1917

    Can America Stay Neutral?

    Wilson asked Americans to be “impartial in thought as well as in action.” He won reelection in 1916 on the slogan “He kept us out of war.” Five months later he asked Congress for one.

    President Wilson addresses Congress · Library of Congress

    Data · May 7, 1915

    Who Was on the Lusitania?

    Germany had warned travelers. The notice on the left ran in New York newspapers on the morning the Lusitania sailed. Below, each dot stands for about ten of the people on board. Sort them to see who came home.

    Read the notice

    “Travellers sailing in the war zone on ships of Great Britain or her allies do so at their own risk.” Imperial German Embassy, April 22, 1915

    Died, 1,198Americans who died, 128Survived, about 760

    Germany said the ship was fair game. It was right that the Lusitania carried war supplies: about 4.2 million rifle cartridges were in its hold. Americans answered that no cargo made it right to drown women and children without warning.

    Wilson sent Germany three angry letters but did not ask for war. In 1916, after another ship was torpedoed, Germany promised to stop sinking passenger ships without warning. That promise is called the Sussex Pledge. It lasted until February 1, 1917.

    Britain wanted to show Wilson the telegram without letting Germany know its codes were broken. So British agents got a second copy from the telegraph office in Mexico City and handed that one over on February 24.

    Americans read it in their newspapers on March 1. Many thought it was a fake until Zimmermann himself admitted it was real two days later.

    The ones who fought under another flag

    The Harlem Hellfighters

    The U.S. Army refused to let most Black soldiers fight beside white Americans. So Pershing lent the 369th Infantry, a Black regiment from New York, to the French Army. The French gave them French helmets and rifles and sent them into the line.

    On the night of May 15, 1918, Private Henry Johnson and Private Needham Roberts were on guard when about two dozen German soldiers raided their post. Johnson was wounded 21 times. When his rifle jammed he fought on with its butt and a bolo knife until the Germans ran. France gave him its highest war medal, the Croix de Guerre. The United States gave him the Medal of Honor in 2015, 86 years after he died.

    The regiment counted 191 days under fire. The Germans called them “Hellfighters.”

    “Make way for Democracy! We saved it in France, and by the Great Jehovah, we will save it in the United States of America, or know the reason why.”

    Film · November 11, 1918

    The Moment the Guns Fell Silent

    Armies used sound ranging machines to find enemy cannons by recording the sound of their blasts. One American machine near the Moselle River was running at 11 a.m. on the last day. This film turns that recording back into sound.

    The armistice was signed just after 5 that morning in a railroad car in the forest near Compiègne. Generals let the fighting run until 11 o’clock. About 2,700 men on all sides died on that last morning.

    Chapter IV · The home front, 1917 to 1919

    The War at Home

    To fight in France, the government reached into every American town. It drafted the soldiers, sold the bonds, wrote the posters, and decided which words were a crime.

    Selling the war

    Uncle Sam wants you

    The Selective Service Act of May 1917 required men to register for the draft. About 24 million signed up and 2.8 million were drafted.

    A new agency, the Committee on Public Information, was run by a journalist named George Creel. It printed posters, made movies, and trained 75,000 volunteer “Four Minute Men” to give short speeches in movie theaters between reels.

    Anything German became suspect. Schools dropped German classes, sauerkraut was renamed “liberty cabbage,” and German Americans were forced to kiss the flag or buy bonds in front of crowds. In April 1918 a mob near St. Louis lynched Robert Prager, a German immigrant, on a rumor that he was disloyal.

    Case files · Espionage Act, 1917 · Sedition Act, 1918

    Arrested for Words

    The Espionage Act made it a crime to interfere with the draft. The Sedition Act of 1918 went further and banned “disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive language” about the government, the flag, or the armed forces. Read what each person said or did. Guess what happened to them.

    What Killed More American Soldiers?

    In March 1918 a cook at Camp Funston, Kansas, reported sick with the flu. By October a deadly strain was moving through every army camp and troop ship. Make your prediction first.

    Killed in battle53,402
    Died of disease, accidents, other causes63,114

    Most of those deaths came from the flu and the pneumonia that followed it. At home, about 675,000 Americans died in the pandemic, more than all the Americans killed in battle in both world wars combined. October 1918 was the deadliest month in American history.

    Map sheet 3 · The United States

    The Great Migration Begins

    The war cut off immigration from Europe just as factories needed more workers. Black families in the South heard about the jobs from letters, railroad recruiters, and newspapers like the Chicago Defender. Between 1916 and 1919 about half a million moved north. Switch between the 1910 and 1920 census to see where they went.

    Black population of northern cities

      Detroit grew fastest of all. Henry Ford had started paying five dollars a day in 1914, and by the middle of the 1920s his River Rouge plant was one of the largest employers of Black workers in the country.

      Michigan’s own

      The Polar Bears

      The 339th Infantry trained at Camp Custer near Battle Creek. Most of its men came from Detroit and the rest of Michigan. In the summer of 1918 they expected to go to France. Instead they landed at Archangel, Russia, on September 4, 1918.

      Britain wanted them there to guard war supplies and fight the Bolsheviks, the communists who had taken over Russia and quit the war. Wilson agreed. The Polar Bears fought through a winter when temperatures fell to 50 below, long after the armistice in France. Families in Michigan wrote to Congress demanding to know why their sons were still fighting. The last of them sailed home in the summer of 1919.

      A white bear statue marks their graves at White Chapel Cemetery in Troy, Michigan.

      The Senate says no

      Article Ten

      A treaty needs two thirds of the Senate. Republican Senator Henry Cabot Lodge of Massachusetts led the opposition. His target was Article 10 of the League of Nations, which promised that members would protect each other’s borders. Lodge said that could drag American soldiers into wars Congress never voted for. A smaller group, the “Irreconcilables,” wanted no treaty at all.

      Wilson refused to accept Lodge’s changes. He set out by train to win over voters directly and gave about 40 speeches in 22 days. On September 25, 1919, he collapsed in Pueblo, Colorado. A week later he suffered a stroke that left him partly paralyzed.

      The Senate voted down the treaty in November 1919. On March 19, 1920, it failed again, 49 to 35, seven votes short. The United States never joined the League of Nations.

      Chapter VI · Your verdict

      Should the Senate Have Signed?

      Wilson believed the League of Nations was the only way to keep a second world war from coming. Lodge believed it would put American soldiers under the control of other countries. Twenty years later, a second world war came.
