01
The game / Four real, one invented

Spot the Con.

Five stories. One of them never happened.

Read each card. Choose Real or Invented. You'll get the answer only after you lock in all five.

This is a test of plausibility, not of intelligence. Nothing here requires prior knowledge. Watch what makes a story feel true.

Your rule

Vote first. Then ask why you were persuaded.

Card A · London, 1822

The Country That Never Existed

Gregor MacGregor claimed to rule Poyais, a wealthy Central American nation. He sold land and bonds. Around 250 settlers sailed toward their new lives—only to find jungle and no functioning country.

1823 the settlement collapses; many colonists die. Exact death totals differ across historical accounts.
Your verdict
Card B · New York, late 1800s–1928

The Man Who Sold the Brooklyn Bridge

George C. Parker offered the Brooklyn Bridge to buyers who thought they could collect tolls. Accounts also attribute sales of Grant's Tomb to him. He was sentenced to life imprisonment after a 1928 fraud conviction.

LIFE Parker died in Sing Sing prison in 1937.
Your verdict
Card C · Netherlands, 1937–1947

The Painting That Fooled Göring

Han van Meegeren painted new works as if they were lost Vermeers. Experts praised his fakes. Nazi leader Hermann Göring acquired one; after World War II, van Meegeren had to prove he was a forger rather than a seller of Dutch national treasures.

ONE FAKE a supposed Vermeer reaches Göring. Exact trade totals vary across accounts.
Your verdict
Card D · Berlin, 1920

The Lost Princess

A woman later known as Anna Anderson claimed to be Anastasia Romanov, surviving daughter of Russia's murdered imperial family. Her supporters argued for decades. DNA evidence later contradicted the claim.

32 years of litigation in German courts, 1938–1970, as summarized in the lesson deck.
Your verdict
Card E · Detroit, 1958

The Miracle in a Bottle

A dealer named Raymond Kessler supposedly sold “Mileage Plus,” a gas additive promising 40% better mileage. After 11,000 bottles, inspectors allegedly found nothing but water and green food coloring.

$2 allegedly charged per bottle.
Your verdict
The table is waiting

0 of 5 decisions made

Change any guess before locking. A verdict can't be edited once revealed.

The reveal
FAKE

Card E was invented.

There was no Raymond Kessler selling Mileage Plus in this lesson's fictional scenario. The most ordinary story was the one we made up.

Notice what your brain did: perhaps the absurd stories felt too absurd to be real. Perhaps the local detail—Detroit, 1958, $2—made Card E sound like a newspaper clipping.

That is the rabbit hole: plausibility is not evidence.

02
Economics flashback / The trap

Match the Bias.

Con artists don't need to invent human weaknesses. They use the shortcuts we already rely on.

Match each case to the best-fitting thinking shortcut used in the original lesson. These categories simplify complicated events; a historical con usually has more than one cause.

A · Herd mentality

Investors saw Poyais bonds in a market already excited about South American opportunities. The crowd made the claim feel safe.

B · Overconfidence

The buyer believed they had found an investment opportunity everyone else had missed.

C · Confirmation bias

An expert already expected Vermeer to have painted biblical scenes. The forgery looked like the missing confirmation.

D · Sunk cost

Supporters had spent years and money on Anderson's story. Changing their minds meant confronting those losses.

Down another level / Psychological research

Who runs
the con?

In 2002, psychologists Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams studied three overlapping but distinct traits: Machiavellianism, narcissism and subclinical psychopathy. Together they became known as the Dark Triad.

These are personality trait constructs, not a license to label people. A trait score is not a diagnosis.

Your first guide / The Cheshire Cat

Machiavellianism

Cold strategy. People as pieces on a board.

The name comes from Niccolò Machiavelli, whose The Prince was written in 1513. In personality research, high Machiavellianism describes a tendency toward calculating manipulation, strategic flattery and distrust.

The trick: control the options so the other person thinks the choice was theirs.

“That depends a good deal on where you want to get to.”Cheshire Cat · Lewis Carroll, 1865

Your second guide / The Queen of Hearts

Narcissism

Craving admiration. Assuming special treatment.

The name reaches back to Narcissus, who fell in love with his reflection. In trait research, narcissism can involve grandiosity, entitlement and extreme sensitivity to criticism.

The trick: make disagreement feel like an insult to someone who deserves absolute loyalty.

“Off with his head!”Queen of Hearts · Lewis Carroll, 1865
Your third guide / The Walrus

Psychopathy

Charm on the surface. Reduced fear or remorse beneath it.

Psychiatrist Hervey Cleckley's The Mask of Sanity (1941) helped shape later descriptions of psychopathic traits. Researchers study callousness, impulsivity, low fear and superficial charm, among other tendencies.

The trick: a sympathetic performance is not proof of empathy.

“I weep for you,” the Walrus said: “I deeply sympathize.”The Walrus · Lewis Carroll, 1871
03
Applied thinking / Fictional situations

Read the Signal.

Look at behaviors, not at a person's supposed diagnosis. These are invented situations designed to highlight the strategy in each description.

Scene 01 / The plan

The rehearsed compliment

Someone flatters a rival before a vote—not because they mean it, but to gain support for a proposal.

Scene 02 / The crown

The untouchable leader

A leader demands praise and treats all criticism as betrayal, even when the evidence is clear.

Scene 03 / The mask

The empty apology

A person gives a convincing apology, then repeatedly takes the same harmful risk without concern for others.

No behavior proves a personality disorder. The examples are deliberately simplified, and all three traits can overlap.

04
The bonus round / 2013

The Fourth Trait.

What if the reward is someone else's suffering?

Researchers Erin Buckels, Daniel Jones and Delroy Paulhus investigated everyday sadism—taking pleasure in cruelty. In one experiment, participants chose among unpleasant tasks including apparently killing named pill bugs.

The setup used a hidden barrier to keep the bugs safe. The question was about the human decision, not actual injury.

26.8% chose the bug-killing task

26.8%

19 of 71 participants in this small experiment—not 26.8% of all people. A further 26.8% chose to assist with the bug task.

Adding sadism gives researchers a fourth construct: the Dark Tetrad.

Before you use a label

The Goldwater Rule.

In 1964, a magazine asked psychiatrists to judge presidential candidate Barry Goldwater without examining him. The controversy helped lead the American Psychiatric Association to adopt a formal ethics rule in 1973.

The APA rule concerns psychiatrists offering professional opinions about public figures they have not examined and without authorization. Our classroom adopts an even simpler rule for discussion:

Observe the actions.
Don't diagnose the person.

We can analyze documented behavior, incentives and consequences. We cannot diagnose historical figures, celebrities or classmates from stories.

05
Final chapter / Your judgment

Climb Out.

You met the con, the mental shortcut and the person who might exploit it. Now practice the habit that breaks the trick.

The habit to keep

Ask for evidence before a verdict.

Confident stories are not proof. Neither are numbers, costumes, official-looking documents or famous names.
