The game · Four real, one invented

Spot the con

Five stories. One of them never happened. Read each card and choose Real or Invented. When all five are in, lock them and the cards turn over to show the record.

This tests what feels believable, not what you already know. Nobody sees your guesses.

Real

Card A · London, 1822

The country that never existed

Gregor MacGregor, a Scottish soldier back from the wars in South America, told London he ruled Poyais, a rich nation on the coast of Central America. He sold its land and its government bonds. In 1822 and 1823 two ships carried about 250 settlers there.

1823the settlers step ashore
Your verdict
The recordReal. They found jungle and no country at all. At least 180 of the settlers died, and fewer than 50 ever made it back to Britain. The portrait on this card comes from MacGregor's own 1822 guidebook for settlers.

The painting that fooled Göring

Han van Meegeren painted new pictures in the style of Johannes Vermeer and sold them as lost masterpieces. Experts praised them. Hermann Göring, second only to Hitler in Nazi Germany, bought one. After the war van Meegeren was arrested for selling a Dutch national treasure to the enemy.

The reveal

Card E was invented.

The made up story was also the least strange one. A fake country, a bridge for sale and a forged Vermeer all sound impossible, and all three happened. Card E had a city, a year, a price and a bottle count, the details a real newspaper story carries.

Specific details can make a story feel true without being evidence for it. Before you trust a story, ask where its details came from.

Economics flashback · The trap

Match the bias

Con artists do not have to invent human weaknesses. They use the shortcuts our brains already take.

Match each real case to the thinking shortcut that fits it best. Real cons usually have more than one cause, so look for the strongest fit.

Down another level · Psychological research

Who runs the con?

In 2002 the psychologists Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams studied three personality traits that overlap but are not the same. Together they became known as the Dark Triad. Tap a circle.

Each trait gets a guide from Lewis Carroll's Wonderland. The guides are metaphors, not diagnoses.

These are personality traits measured on a scale in research. A score is not a diagnosis, and nobody in this lesson is being diagnosed.

Your first guide · The Cheshire Cat

Machiavellianism

Manipulating people on purpose, for advantage.

The name comes from Niccolò Machiavelli, the Florentine diplomat who wrote The Prince in 1513, a handbook on gaining and keeping power. In personality research, high Machiavellianism means a habit of calculated manipulation, strategic flattery and cynical distrust of others.

The tactic: control the options so the other person thinks the choice was theirs.

That depends a good deal on where you want to get to.The Cheshire Cat, Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, Lewis Carroll, 1865

Your second guide · The Queen of Hearts

Narcissism

A hunger for admiration and special treatment.

The name reaches back to the Greek myth of Narcissus, a young man who fell in love with his own reflection in a pool. In trait research, narcissism can include grandiosity, entitlement and an explosive reaction to criticism.

The tactic: make disagreement feel like disloyalty to someone who deserves absolute devotion.

Off with his head!The Queen of Hearts, Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, Lewis Carroll, 1865

Your third guide · The Walrus

Psychopathy

Surface charm with little fear or remorse.

The psychiatrist Hervey Cleckley's 1941 book The Mask of Sanity shaped how later researchers described psychopathic traits. Researchers study callousness, impulsiveness, low fear and shallow charm, among other tendencies.

In the poem, the Walrus weeps over the oysters while he eats every one of them.

The tactic: a sympathetic performance is not proof of sympathy.

I weep for you, the Walrus said: I deeply sympathize."The Walrus and the Carpenter," Through the Looking Glass, Lewis Carroll, 1871
04

Applied thinking · Invented situations

Read the signal

Look at the behavior, not at a guess about the person. These scenes are invented to spotlight one tactic each.

Scene 01 · The plan

The rehearsed compliment

Someone flatters a rival before a vote. They do not mean a word of it. They want support for their own proposal.

Scene 02 · The crown

The untouchable leader

A leader demands praise and treats every criticism as betrayal, even when the evidence against them is clear.

Scene 03 · The mask

The empty apology

A person gives a moving apology, then takes the same dangerous risk again and again without concern for who gets hurt.

No single behavior proves a personality disorder. These examples are simplified on purpose, and the traits overlap.

The bonus round · 2013

The fourth trait

What if the reward is someone else's suffering?

Erin Buckels, Daniel Jones and Delroy Paulhus studied everyday sadism, taking pleasure in cruelty. In one experiment, 71 participants chose one of four unpleasant jobs. One job was feeding live pill bugs into a modified coffee grinder. The bugs had names.

MuffinIkeTootsie

A hidden barrier kept every bug safe. The grinder made a crunching sound anyway. The study measured the human choice, not real harm.

26.8%

chose to kill the bugs. That is 19 of 71 people in one small study, not 26.8 percent of everyone.

People who scored higher on sadism were more likely to pick the bug job. Add sadism to the triad and researchers call it the Dark Tetrad.

06

Before you use a label · 1964

The Goldwater rule

In July 1964 Fact magazine mailed one question to 12,356 psychiatrists: was Barry Goldwater psychologically fit to be president?

12,356psychiatrists who got the survey
2,417mailed an answer back
1,189said he was unfit, the number on the magazine's cover
657said he was fit
571said they did not know enough to say
0

None of them had met him as a patient. Goldwater lost the election, then sued the magazine's publisher for libel. In 1968 a jury awarded him $1 in damages and $75,000 in punitive damages, and an appeals court upheld it.

In 1973 the American Psychiatric Association made it an ethics rule: psychiatrists should not give a professional opinion about a public figure they have not examined and who has not given permission.

Observe the actions. Do not diagnose the person.

We can study documented behavior, incentives and consequences. We do not diagnose historical figures, celebrities or classmates from stories.

07

Final chapter · Your judgment

Climb out

You met the con, the shortcut it used and the kind of person who might run it. Now practice the habit that breaks the trick.

The habit to keep

Ask for evidence before a verdict.

Confident stories are not proof. Neither are exact numbers, uniforms, official looking paper or famous names. MacGregor had all of them.

A good investigator checks the record
