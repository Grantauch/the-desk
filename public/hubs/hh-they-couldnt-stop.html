Hidden History · Mind Games · Down the rabbit hole

They couldn't stop

Can fear spread from one mind to the next like a disease?

Five outbreaks. Five centuries. A city that danced, a town that smelled gas, a school that laughed, a generation that twitched, and a case the government still cannot close. Every level goes deeper.

Rule for the descent: nobody in this story was faking. Watch for what made it real.

Level one

Strasbourg · July to September 1518

The summer the city danced

In the middle of July 1518, a woman stepped into a narrow street in Strasbourg and began to dance. There was no music and no festival. Chroniclers later named her Frau Troffea. She kept going for days.

Then her neighbors joined. Strasbourg was a free city of the Holy Roman Empire, and its people were hungry. Years of bad harvests had pushed bread prices up, and smallpox and a new disease called syphilis were spreading. Many believed a saint named Vitus could curse sinners with a dance they could not stop.

The city council asked its doctors. The doctors said the dancers had overheated blood and needed to dance it out. So the city cleared the grain market, built a wooden stage and paid musicians to keep them moving.

Name the trap

The nocebo effect

A placebo makes you feel better because you expect it to. A nocebo makes you feel worse because you expect it to. If you believe a saint can curse you to dance, the belief can shape what your body does.

Level two

Mattoon, Illinois · September 1944

The gas that was never there

Late on the night of September 1, 1944, Aline Kearney of Mattoon, a small railroad town in Illinois, smelled something sweet in her bedroom. Her legs went numb and her throat burned. Her husband later saw a tall figure at the window.

The next day the town's newspaper, the Daily Journal-Gazette, put her on the front page and called her the first victim. Within days police were taking reports from all over town. Armed men patrolled the streets. State police came in. Nobody found a gasser or a single trace of gas. Then the headlines changed, and so did the town.

Name the trap

The availability heuristic

We judge how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind. When the gasser is on every front page, every strange smell becomes the gasser.

Level three

Kashasha, Tanganyika · January 1962

The school that could not stop laughing

On January 30, 1962, at a girls' boarding school run by missionaries near Lake Victoria, three students started laughing and could not stop. Within weeks 95 of the school's 159 girls were affected. The attacks lasted hours, sometimes days, and came with crying and fainting. The teachers never caught it.

Tanganyika had won its independence from Britain seven weeks earlier. Students at mission schools lived between strict new expectations and the world they came from. When the school closed on March 18 and sent the girls home, the laughing went with them.

Name the trap

The bandwagon effect

We do things because the people around us are doing them. In a crowded dorm, the people around you are everyone you know.

Level four

Germany, the UK and the US · 2019 to 2021

The first outbreak spread by screen

In February 2019 a young German man launched a YouTube channel called Gewitter im Kopf, Thunderstorm in the Head, about his Tourette syndrome. It grew past two million subscribers. In 2020 and 2021, while millions of teenagers were stuck at home, doctors in Germany, Britain, Canada and the United States saw a wave of young patients with sudden, dramatic tics.

The new patients often had the same movements and shouted the same odd phrases as the influencers they watched. In Hannover, Dr. Kirsten Müller-Vahl's team named it in August 2021: mass social media induced illness, the first outbreak of its kind spread only through screens. Some patients improved quickly once they understood what was happening.

1962 · person to person

Each case has to see the last one. Distance slows it down.

2021 · one screen to everyone

One channel reaches millions at once. Distance stops mattering.

Not faking. Doctors call this a functional neurological disorder. The brain is healthy in structure, but the way it sends signals has gone wrong. The tics are real and the person cannot simply stop them. The cause is the software, not the hardware.

Name the trap

Social proof

When we are unsure what is happening to us, we copy what we see other people do. A feed full of tics can teach a stressed brain a new script.

Level five

Havana, Cuba · 2016 to today

The case that is still open

In late 2016 American diplomats in Havana began reporting a piercing sound, pressure in their heads, then dizziness, ringing ears and trouble thinking. The State Department pulled most of its staff from Cuba in 2017. Over the next years hundreds of American diplomats, spies and soldiers in more than a dozen countries reported similar episodes. The press called it Havana Syndrome.

Name the trap

Confirmation bias

Both sides have it. If you already suspect an enemy weapon, every headache is proof. If you already suspect mass hysteria, every patient is imagining it. The evidence has to do the work.
