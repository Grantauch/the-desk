The true story of the girls who glowed

Shines inthe Dark

From the real record
Illustration made from a photograph of the United States Radium Corporation studio, about 1922 · Argonne National Laboratory

In 1917 a company in Orange, New Jersey, hired young women to paint glowing numbers on watch dials with radium. Its ads promised the paint shines in the dark. The painters shaped their brushes between their lips hundreds of times a day. Ten years later their jaws were falling apart, and the company still said the work was safe.

Page 1Orange, New Jersey · 1917 to 1920

The Magic Paint

Sabin von Sochocky was a doctor who came to the United States from Austria in 1906. By 1913 he had a paint that glowed on its own, a pinch of radium mixed into zinc sulfide, a powder that flashes whenever radiation hits it.

In 1917 his company moved into a bigger plant in Orange, New Jersey. The country had just entered the First World War, and soldiers needed watches they could read at night. By 1920 Scientific American counted more than 1,000,000 glowing watches and clocks.

The painting was done by young women. Katherine Schaub started on February 1, 1917, when she was 14. Grace Fryer started that year at 18. They were paid by the dial, so speed was money.

The camel hair brushes spread after a few strokes, and a fat brush blotted the tiny numbers. The fastest fix was to draw the tip between your lips. The painters called the routine lip, dip, paint.

“Miss Fryer estimates that she put the radium paint brush to her lips to wet it about 300 times a day between 1917 and 1920 when she and 70 other women were employed.”

United Press interview, May 15, 1928

Page 2Orange, New Jersey · 1922 to 1924

Something in the Jaw

Mollie Maggia had painted dials since 1917. Early in 1922 a dentist pulled a sore tooth, and the hole never healed. More teeth came out, then pieces of her jawbone. She died on September 12, 1922. She was 24, and the story that followed her to the grave was that she had died of syphilis.

“We have a chronic, progressive rotting away of the bone.”

Castle, Drinker and Drinker, Journal of Industrial Hygiene, 1925

More painters were getting sick the same way. In March 1924 the company president, Arthur Roeder, hired a Harvard team led by the husband and wife doctors Cecil and Katherine Drinker. The company's first guess was dirty brushes. The Drinkers cultured them and grew nothing but ordinary mouth germs.

So they looked at the paint. They took the painters into a darkroom and flashed a light. Zinc sulfide gives light back slowly, so any paint dust would glow for a few seconds afterward.

“The hair, faces, hands, arms, necks, the dresses, the underclothes, even the corsets, of the dial painters were luminous. One of the girls showed luminous spots on her legs and thighs. The back of another was luminous almost to her waist.”

Castle, Drinker and Drinker, 1925

The man who weighed out the powder said it blew away if he breathed on it. The woman who had the job before him noticed that when she blew her nose, it glowed in her handkerchief.

Glow proves dust, not radiation. For that the Drinkers placed sealed dental X ray film around the building. Film in a sealed packet only clouds if radiation passes through the wrapper. The radiologist George Pfahler had set the rule. If a sealed film fogs within two weeks, the people near it are getting too much.

    They also drew blood from 22 employees. Not one sample, the report said, was “entirely normal.” On June 3, 1924, the Drinkers told Roeder what they believed. Radium had caused the dying jaws.

    The Report That Changed on the Way

    Roeder did not accept the answer. He blamed infections the women had picked up somewhere else. Then the report reached the New Jersey Department of Labor saying something different. In the version the state received, every girl was in perfect condition.

    Alice Hamilton, the country's leading expert on factory poisons, heard about it and wrote to Katherine Drinker.

    “Do you suppose Roeder could do such a thing as to issue a forged report in your name?”

    Alice Hamilton to Katherine Drinker, 1925

    The company had asked the Drinkers to hold off publishing because of the lawsuits. Roeder threatened to sue if they went ahead. They published anyway, in the August 1925 Journal of Industrial Hygiene, and the real conclusion went into print.

    Outsiders were counting too. Frederick Hoffman, a Prudential Insurance statistician brought in by the Consumers' League, told the American Medical Association in May 1925 about 5 dead painters and 12 living ones with the same rotting jaws. He named it radium necrosis.

    Harrison Martland, the Essex County medical examiner, picked up gamma rays coming out of living painters and found radon, the gas radium gives off, in their breath. He laid bone from a dead painter on dental film, and the bone took its own picture.

    The company hired its own expert, Frederick Flinn of Columbia University. His 1926 paper found no industrial hazard. Two years and several deaths later, he changed his mind in print.

    Page 4Newark courts · 1927 to 1928

    Doomed to Die

    In May 1927 Grace Fryer found a lawyer who would take the case, Raymond Berry of Newark. Katherine Schaub and Edna Hussman joined her, with Mollie Maggia's sisters Quinta McDonald and Albina Larice. Each sued the United States Radium Corporation for $250,000.

    The company's answer was the calendar. New Jersey gave workers two years to sue. The women had painted from 1917 to 1920 and were not diagnosed until 1927, so the company argued their time ran out before they knew what was wrong.

    In October 1927 Mollie Maggia's body was dug up to settle how she had died. Doctors found no sign of syphilis. Her bones were radioactive.

    Doomed to Die, Tells Emotions

    Two weeks later Roeder wrote that settling admitted nothing, because “there is very little question but that we had a perfect defense.”

    In November 1928 Sabin von Sochocky, the man who brought radium paint to America, died of radium poisoning. He had once figured the radium in his own body would take 3,520 years to leave it. Grace Fryer died on October 27, 1933. She was 34.

    Meanwhile, at the country club

    Rich people paid to swallow radium. Eben Byers, a Pittsburgh steel man and the 1906 national amateur golf champion, hurt his arm in 1927, and a doctor suggested Radithor, bottled radium water from East Orange, New Jersey. Byers drank about three bottles a day for two years.

    He died on March 31, 1932, at 51, after surgeons had removed most of his jaw. Laid on film for ten days, his teeth took their own picture.

    Page 5Ottawa, Illinois · 1922 to 1939

    The Society of the Living Dead

    Radium Dial opened a studio in an old high school in Ottawa, Illinois, in 1922. In 1925 a company officer told a federal investigator it turned out about 4,300 dials a day, and that it was practically impossible to stop the workers from tipping their brushes in their mouths.

    In 1928 the New Jersey headlines reached Illinois, and a notice went up in the plant. According to the women's lawyer, it said Ottawa used a different type of radium that was not dangerous.

    Catherine Wolfe painted there from 1922 to 1931 for $15 a week. By 1931 she walked with a limp, and her supervisors let her go.

    “Your limping will cause talk. We're sorry, you'll have to go.”

    As Catherine Donohue testified, February 1938

    She married Tom Donohue in 1932 and had two children as her health failed. In 1937 a Chicago lawyer, Leonard Grossman, took her case and other Ottawa painters' cases without pay. The newspapers called the group the Society of the Living Dead.

    Hearing Taken to Bedside of Doomed Woman

    “In my opinion, her death is almost certain within a matter of months,” Dr. Dalitsch testified in her absence.

    Evening Star, Associated Press · February 11, 1938
    Radium Victim Dies as Doctor Predicted

    She was the 10th to die. Four doctors certified that she died of radium poisoning.

    Washington Daily News, United Press · July 27, 1938

    The hearing opened on February 10, 1938. When a dentist testified that she would be dead within months, Catherine Donohue collapsed. She weighed 71 pounds. The arbitrator moved the rest of the hearing to the couch in her living room.

    She won. The Illinois Industrial Commission ordered Radium Dial to pay her $5,661. The company, which had already moved to New York, appealed and fought even the bond it had to post to keep appealing. Catherine Donohue died on July 27, 1938, at 35. The company's last appeals failed in 1939.

    Page 6What the bones taught · 1931 to today

    What the Bones Taught

    By 1931 Martland was seeing bone cancers appear years after the jaws had rotted. The dial painters, he wrote, were “proof that radiation can produce malignancy.” It was the first hard evidence that radiation stored inside the body causes cancer.

    The physicist Robley Evans built counters that could measure radium inside living people. In 1941, with the military about to order glowing dials by the thousands, a federal committee reviewed 27 people Evans had measured. Seven held half a microcurie or less and had no symptoms. The twenty who were sick held 1.2 to 23. The committee set the limit for a worker's body at 0.1.

    For radium, one microcurie is about one millionth of a gram.

    Evans' CounterListen to what the women carried
    Pick a measurement
    Each button holds a real measurement of radium in a human body. Turn on sound at the bottom corner to hear the clicks.

    The red band on the meter is everything above the 1941 limit. The clicks are scaled so the loudest one stays bearable. Each time the click rate triples, the radium went up about ten times.

    The simplest lesson was to keep the brush out of your mouth. After 1925 the lip pointing stopped. Decades later Argonne National Laboratory measured how much radium each generation of painters had absorbed.
