Two Sources

Thousands of people chased one rumor for three weeks. Two reporters built one case for twenty-six months. The difference was not intelligence or effort. It was a rule.

WKNR · DETROIT
12 OCT 1969

A phone call reaches a city.

A caller got through to Russ Gibb, a disc jockey at WKNR. He said Paul McCartney was dead, the Beatles had hidden it, and their records contained the proof.

The caller told Gibb to play a track backward. Gibb did it live. His signal reached tens of thousands of people at the same time from a voice many heard every day.

Within about three weeks, newspapers and television stations across the country were running the story.

Why a phone call could do that

Information had fewer doors in 1969. No internet. No phone in your pocket. A handful of television channels, a local paper, and radio. A disc jockey with a powerful signal stood in one of those doors.

The closest modern comparison is a huge account posting something at nine in the morning. But the comparison breaks in an important way. Online, a reply can challenge the post where everybody sees it. In 1969 the radio talked and you listened. Asking a friend who heard the same broadcast was not independent checking. It was one signal arriving through two radios.

MICHIGAN DAILY
14 OCT 1969

The clue list gets an author.

Two days later, a University of Michigan student named Fred LaBour published a piece announcing McCartney's death and listing “new evidence.”

LaBour says he invented most of the clues as satire. His original readers had context. When stations and other papers repeated the piece, the context fell away. What reached another city sounded like “the Michigan Daily reported,” not “a student wrote a joke.”

The article gave people a shared checklist. From then on, thousands were not independently discovering clues. They were checking the same list.

How a joke stops being a joke

A piece of writing changes when it leaves the room where it was written. Reprint a student humor piece in a newspaper. Read one line on the radio. Share a screenshot without the account name. Each move can leave a little context behind.

Nobody has to lie. The sentence simply travels faster than the frame that told you how seriously to take it. By the time the clue list reached California, the original tone had vanished. A joke had become a report because the label survived and the setting did not.

SOURCE TRACE
RUN BACKWARD

Ten thousand people. One source.

People in Cleveland and Sacramento found the same clues without knowing one another. That looked like corroboration because the results matched.

The missing step was tracing where each person got the checklist. Agreement counts as independent only when the sources could have gotten it wrong separately.

What independent actually means

Two students turn in the same wrong answer. Maybe the question fooled both of them. Maybe one copied. You cannot decide by staring at the answer. You have to find out where each answer came from.

Two sources count as two only when they could have failed separately. If both people read the same article, you have one article and two readers. Trace the famous clue hunt backward and thousands of branches return to the same short week in Michigan. The crowd was real. The work was real. The independent source count stayed tiny.

WATERGATE
17 JUN 1972

Five men inside an office.

At about two in the morning, five men were arrested inside the Democratic National Committee offices in Washington's Watergate complex.

The Democratic National Committee runs the Democratic Party's national campaign operation. Breaking into it during a presidential election was not an ordinary office burglary. The men carried cameras and electronic surveillance equipment, not things they could sell.

The next morning it was a small story. It took twenty-six months for the case to help end a presidency.

Why this took two years and the rumor took three weeks

The rumor moved whenever somebody repeated it. Repetition is fast. Nothing has to be checked and every retelling feels like progress.

Verified reporting moves only when a claim is confirmed. Learn something Tuesday. Believe it. Still do not print it. Spend Wednesday finding a second person who knows it independently. On Thursday that person refuses to be quoted, so you start again. Now repeat that for every sentence that carries the case.

The reporters were not slow. They were only allowed to move when something held.

NEWSROOM STANDARD
LOCK SIGNAL

The rule.

If information came from a source who would not be named, the Washington Post did not run it until a second source, independent of the first, confirmed it.

Two people who heard the same third person are one source and an echo. A person who agrees after you reveal what you already know may only be agreeing with you.

The rule killed stories reporters believed were true. That cost was the point. A standard matters because it can stop you from doing what you want.

Why use a source who will not be named?

The people who know what happened inside a powerful organization usually work there. Speaking publicly may cost a job or worse. If newspapers printed only what named officials volunteered, almost nothing about the inside of government would reach the public.

An anonymous source is therefore useful and risky at once. The two-source rule is the safety system attached to that tool. Protecting a name does not mean skipping confirmation. One unnamed person is still one person.

RECORD WORK
1972–1973

Not a garage. Paper.

The popular picture is a shadowed parking garage and a source in a trench coat. The actual work was campaign filings, phone records, payrolls, and repeat interviews.

A record holds still. A phone log shows the same call every time you look. That makes a witness more useful because the question can become exact: why did this number call that number on this date?

On July 16, 1973, White House staffer Alexander Butterfield answered a routine Senate question and confirmed that a taping system had recorded the president's conversations. Nobody had started the question looking for a taping system. Thorough work made room for the answer.

SAME TEST
BOTH CLAIMS

What would have killed it?

Do not begin with “Do I believe this?” Ask what finding would end the claim, then see whether that finding exists.

A living Paul McCartney should have ended the death rumor. He was alive, photographed, and interviewed. The rumor absorbed that evidence.

The Watergate case could have died if financial and communication records failed to connect the burglars to the campaign. Investigators obtained the records. They showed the opposite.

A claim that cannot lose is not winning

Imagine somebody claims an invisible dog is in the room. It makes no sound, leaves no fur, and cannot be touched. You cannot prove the dog is absent because the claim removed every possible test.

That does not make the claim strong. It makes the claim uncheckable. The useful question is what would have to show up for me to drop this belief. Name it before you search. If no possible finding could change your mind, you are not testing the world. You are measuring your own confidence.

Why a bigger crowd found less

More people find more when they search different places. Ten thousand people checking the same twelve clues are not ten thousand investigations. They are one procedure repeated ten thousand times.

All that work creates volume, and volume feels like evidence because a claim arrives from a hundred directions. Trace those directions backward and they may still lead to one room. Two reporters could beat the crowd because they kept asking where each fact came from and whether the second source could have failed separately.

The next three weeks run on three questions.

  1. Where did this person get it?Not only what did they say. Who told them, and who told that person?
  2. Could these two sources fail separately?If both read the same post, they are not independent.
  3. What would change your mind?Name it before searching so the test does not move after the evidence arrives.

Two independent sources. The method is simple. Following it when a good story is waiting is the hard part.
