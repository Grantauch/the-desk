BATTLES OF THE WESTERN FRONT · 1914 TO 1918

The Line

For more than three years, two armies faced each other across a strip of torn ground that ran over 400 miles, from the North Sea coast of Belgium to the border of Switzerland. Millions of men died trying to push it a few miles in either direction.

August 1914

The Swing

Germany’s war plan, built by General Alfred von Schlieffen, depended on speed. German armies would swing through neutral Belgium, sweep around Paris, and knock France out of the war in about six weeks. Then they would turn east to fight Russia.

On August 4, 1914, German troops crossed into Belgium. Britain had promised to protect Belgian neutrality and declared war that same day. Belgium fought back harder than Germany expected. The forts around Liège held out for twelve days, and German soldiers took revenge on civilians. In the town of Dinant, they executed 674 men, women and children on August 23.

The red arrows show the German advance. The dashed red line marks the deepest point it reached.

September 5 to 12, 1914

The Marne

By early September, German soldiers were within about 30 miles of Paris. The French government packed up and left the city. Then French pilots flying over the front spotted a gap opening between two German armies, and the Allies attacked into it.

Paris sent reinforcements to the battle in about 600 city taxis, which carried several thousand soldiers through the night. After a week of fighting, the Germans pulled back about 40 miles to high ground above the Aisne River and began to dig.

40 miThe German retreat after the Marne. Hardly any battle on this front would move the line that far again until 1918.
Winter 1914

The Line Freezes

Each side tried to get around the other’s northern end, and the two armies leapfrogged toward the coast in what became known as the Race to the Sea. Neither side won the race. By late November 1914 a continuous line of trenches ran from the Belgian coast to Switzerland.

On Christmas Eve, along parts of the British front, German soldiers set candles on their trench walls and sang carols. The next morning men from both sides climbed out, shook hands in no man’s land and traded cigarettes. In some places they helped bury each other’s dead. In a few places they played football. By some estimates around 100,000 soldiers took part. Commanders made sure it never happened on that scale again.

The shaded ground east of the line is held by the German army. Notice that it includes almost all of Belgium and a large piece of northern France.

April 22, 1915

Gas at Ypres

The Belgian town of Ypres sat in a bulge in the Allied line, which meant German guns could fire into it from three sides. It was here that the German army released chlorine gas for the first time, opening a hole four miles wide in the line.

Canadian troops, many of them in combat for the first time, helped plug the gap, some of them holding cloths soaked in water or urine over their faces. Fifteen days later, a U-boat sank the Lusitania. The war was spreading to the sea, but the line on land had not moved.

February 21 to December 18, 1916

Verdun

The German commander Erich von Falkenhayn picked Verdun because he believed France would never give up the ancient fortress city. He planned to force France to keep feeding soldiers into Verdun until its army could not replace them. More than 1,200 German guns opened fire on the morning of February 21.

France held. Nearly every unit in the French army took a turn at Verdun, brought up by trucks along a single supply road that ran day and night. French troops took up the cry Ils ne passeront pas, They shall not pass. The battle lasted 303 days. When it ended, the line sat almost where it had started.

For days I have seen nothing but the most terrible things that can be painted from the human mind.Franz Marc, German painter and soldier, killed at Verdun on March 4, 1916
~700,000French and German soldiers killed, wounded or missing at Verdun.
July 1 to November 18, 1916

The Somme

The British and French attacked along the Somme River partly to take pressure off Verdun. Many of the British soldiers were volunteers in Pals Battalions, men from the same town or the same factory who had signed up together. Press a time to walk through the first morning.

The battle went on for 141 more days. Tanks appeared for the first time in September. When winter shut it down, the Allies had gained about 7 miles at the deepest point, and more than a million men on both sides had been killed or wounded.

1917

Mud

In March 1917 the German army did something unusual. It pulled back as much as 25 miles in one sector to a new, heavily fortified position called the Hindenburg Line, wrecking every road and village it left behind. The faint dashed line shows where the front had been.

In April, the United States declared war, but American troops would not arrive in large numbers for another year. Meanwhile a failed French offensive led to mutinies, with thousands of French soldiers refusing to attack. At Passchendaele, near Ypres, the British attacked from July to November through some of the heaviest rain in years. Shell holes filled with water, and wounded men slipped into them and drowned.

I died in hell—
(They called it Passchendaele).Siegfried Sassoon, British officer and poet, from "Memorial Tablet," 1918
March to July 1918

The Last Gamble

Russia quit the war in March 1918 after its revolution. That freed about 50 German divisions to move west. Germany’s commanders knew American troops were coming, so they threw everything into one last attack before the Americans could tip the balance.

Starting March 21, the German offensives broke through and advanced as much as 40 miles, the biggest moves on this front since 1914. By June they were back on the Marne. At Belleau Wood, U.S. Marines were sent to plug the line. When a French officer advised them to fall back, Marine Captain Lloyd Williams answered with a line Marines still repeat.

Retreat? Hell, we just got here!Captain Lloyd W. Williams, U.S. Marine Corps, Belleau Wood, June 1918
June 6The Marines lost 1,087 men at Belleau Wood on June 6, 1918, their worst single day until the Battle of Tarawa in 1943.
August 8 to November 11, 1918

The Hundred Days

On August 8, Allied tanks and infantry broke through near Amiens. The German commander Erich Ludendorff called it the black day of the German army. From then on the Allies attacked somewhere almost every day.

On September 26, more than a million American soldiers opened the Meuse-Argonne offensive, the largest battle in American history. It cost 26,277 American lives in 47 days. Germany’s allies collapsed one by one, the Kaiser gave up his throne on November 9, and the armistice took effect at 11 a.m. on November 11, 1918.

Look where the line sits on that last morning. German soldiers were still standing on French and Belgian soil. Many Germans refused to believe their army had really been beaten, and that idea would help Adolf Hitler rise to power fifteen years later.

Off the map

Meanwhile, Elsewhere

The Western Front was not the whole war. Three battles far from France changed what happened on it.

EASTERN FRONT

Tannenberg

AUGUST 26 TO 30, 1914 · EAST PRUSSIA

Russia attacked Germany far sooner than the Schlieffen Plan expected. Russian officers sent radio orders without code, and German listeners read every one. The Germans surrounded and destroyed the Russian Second Army, capturing about 92,000 prisoners. Germany still had to keep a huge army in the east for three more years.

OTTOMAN EMPIRE

Gallipoli

APRIL 1915 TO JANUARY 1916 · TURKEY

British, French, Australian and New Zealand troops landed on the Gallipoli peninsula to open a sea route to Russia. Ottoman soldiers pinned them to the beaches for eight months. A young Ottoman officer named Mustafa Kemal made his name here, and later founded modern Turkey. The Allies evacuated after roughly 300,000 casualties.

NORTH SEA

Jutland

MAY 31 TO JUNE 1, 1916

About 250 British and German warships fought the only full battleship clash of the war. Britain lost 14 ships and Germany lost 11, and about 8,600 sailors died. Germany’s fleet never seriously challenged the British blockade again. It turned to U-boats instead, the choice that eventually brought the United States into the war.
