Unions & Collective Bargaining

A tiny secret labor society becomes the largest labor organization in the country, defeats one of America’s richest railroad owners, grows almost sevenfold—and then collapses with astonishing speed.

What changes when workers stop negotiating as individuals and begin acting together?

I
ORIGINS

Why organize at all?

Before the Knights become powerful, the first thing to understand is the problem they were trying to solve: one worker had little leverage, and the law gave organized labor very little protection.

PHILADELPHIA · DECEMBER 1869

A movement starts in the dark.

The Knights of Labor began when Uriah Stephens and eight other Philadelphia garment cutters formed a secret labor organization. Secrecy made sense in a world where organizing could cost a worker his job.

The organization eventually tried something much bigger than protecting one trade: unite people divided by job, skill, race, sex, and region into one national movement.

The artwork is an illustrative reconstruction. Historical accounts most commonly identify nine founders in total: Stephens plus eight other garment cutters.
THE BASIC LOGIC

One worker is replaceable.

A single employee can be fired or replaced without stopping an entire operation. Collective action changes the employer’s problem. Replacing a large share of a workforce at once requires time, recruiting, training, supervision, and money.

That is the logic behind collective bargaining: workers negotiate as a group rather than as isolated individuals. But solidarity immediately creates another question—who belongs in the group?

1 worker — An individual personnel problem.

What happens operationally
The line keeps running. One machine stands idle until a replacement is hired.
The employer’s problem
Hire one person. The cost is a day of output and an afternoon of paperwork.
The workers’ problem
The entire cost of refusing falls on one household.
In this story
C. A. Hall was one worker. On February 18, 1886 the Texas & Pacific fired him after a Knights meeting — a decision the company could make on its own.

10 workers — A disruption an employer may still absorb.

What happens operationally
One crew stops. Other shifts and nearby shops cover the gap.
The employer’s problem
Recruit ten replacements, or simply wait the ten out.
The workers’ problem
Small enough that every striker can be named, replaced, and refused work elsewhere.
In this story
This is the scale at which a single local assembly acts alone — and the scale at which Powderly usually preferred arbitration to a strike.

100 workers — Replacement now requires real recruiting and coordination.

What happens operationally
A whole shop stops, and the work that depends on that shop backs up behind it.
The employer’s problem
Finding, training and supervising 100 replacements costs weeks and wages before it produces anything.
The workers’ problem
It now takes a strike fund. Families can run out of money before the company runs out of patience.
In this story
Local Knights assemblies at roughly this scale won recognition from individual employers through the early 1880s.

1,000 workers — Operations become difficult to sustain without an organized response.

What happens operationally
Entire facilities go quiet and schedules slip across the whole company.
The employer’s problem
Replacement becomes a hiring campaign — and it pulls in courts, guards, and newspapers.
The workers’ problem
Coordination becomes the hard problem: a thousand people have to hold one position for weeks.
In this story
Roughly the scale that stopped traffic on Jay Gould’s Wabash system in 1885 and forced a settlement.

10,000 workers — The dispute becomes a system-level problem, not one personnel decision.

What happens operationally
A network stops. Freight, repair shops, and every connecting line stop with it.
The employer’s problem
You cannot replace ten thousand people across five states quickly. You negotiate — or you bring in force.
The workers’ problem
The leverage is real, and so is the exposure: injunctions, replacement labor, and armed enforcement all arrive at this scale.
In this story
The 1886 Great Southwest strike reached this scale across five states and still lost. Scale created the leverage; it could not by itself keep the trains from moving.

Nothing protected them.

01No federal right forcing an employer to recognize a union.
02No federal requirement that an employer bargain with workers’ chosen representatives.
03Union membership or organizing activity could cost a worker his job.
1935 · National Labor Relations ActDecades later, federal law would protect organizing and collective bargaining for many private-sector workers. The Knights operated in a different legal world.
II
THE KNIGHTS OF LABOR

A union—and a social vision.

The Knights did not simply want a higher paycheck. They imagined a different relationship between workers, ownership, citizenship, and economic power.

GRAND MASTER WORKMAN

Terence Powderly

Powderly led the Knights from 1879 to 1893. He favored education, arbitration, cooperation, and reform more than constant striking. That caution became a central tension inside an organization whose local members sometimes wanted direct confrontation.

1878Elected mayor of Scranton, Pennsylvania.

MEMBERSHIP

Both halves are true.

UNUSUALLY BROAD FOR ITS ERA

Who could enter

Women joined. Black workers joined. Skilled and unskilled workers joined. The Knights deliberately reached across divisions many nineteenth-century labor organizations treated as permanent.

THE LIMITS MATTER TOO

Who was still left out

Equality was inconsistent in practice; many local assemblies were segregated. The Knights excluded Chinese workers and also barred occupations they considered “nonproductive” or socially harmful, commonly including doctors, lawyers, bankers, liquor dealers, and gamblers.

BEYOND A PAY RAISE

What they actually wanted.

The Knights imagined workers gaining more control over economic life—not merely negotiating a slightly better wage inside the same arrangement.

Education.Mutual aid.Cooperation.Eight hours.

Their program also included worker cooperatives, restrictions on child and convict labor, equal pay for equal work, and reforms aimed at concentrated corporate power.

III
RISE · 1885–1886

Winning changes everything.

A victory against Jay Gould makes the Knights look capable of defeating national corporate power. Workers rush to join. Success creates strength—and a new problem of scale.

WABASH SYSTEM · 1885

They beat Jay Gould.

Workers affiliated with the Knights won a settlement against railroads controlled by one of the most powerful financiers in America. Earlier wage levels were restored, and striking workers could return without being punished simply for taking part.

A railroad makes money by moving. In 1885, the trains stopped.

MARSHALL, TEXAS · FEBRUARY 18, 1886

One firing becomes a five-state strike.

C. A. Hall, a Knights member at the Texas & Pacific shops in Marshall, was fired after attending a Knights meeting on company time. The union believed the firing violated the labor agreement won the year before.

IV
COLLAPSE · 1886

The trains keep moving.

The visual logic flips. In 1885, stopped trains meant union leverage. In 1886, trains continue running—and the Knights cannot force the railroad system to stop.

THE GREAT SOUTHWEST STRIKE · 1886

It was over by April.

Railroad brotherhoods did not fully join the strike, so trains kept running. Replacement workers, federal injunctions, armed deputies, railroad security, and state forces increased the pressure. Once the railroad could keep operating, time favored the company.

1885: stopped trains = leverage.
1886: moving trains = defeat.
CHICAGO · MAY 4, 1886

Then came Haymarket.

A labor rally at Haymarket Square ended catastrophically after a bomb was thrown as police moved in. The Knights of Labor did not organize or control the meeting.

Public reaction did not carefully distinguish among unions, anarchists, socialists, radical activists, and different labor organizations. The largest labor organization in the country absorbed part of the backlash while already weakened by the failed railroad strike.

V
LEGACY

Another model survives.

The Knights tried to unite a very broad workforce around sweeping reform. The American Federation of Labor chose a narrower craft-union structure and a tighter workplace focus.

DECEMBER 1886

The other model.

The AFL, founded in 1886 with Samuel Gompers as its first president, organized mainly through autonomous craft unions. That narrower structure could be easier to coordinate, but it also left many less-skilled and marginalized workers outside much of the early federation’s power.

THE FACTUAL SPINE

L014 Note Repair

The cinematic layer disappears here. What remains is the compact sequence students should be able to reconstruct after moving through the story.

  • Knights of Labor founded in Philadelphia in 1869 by Uriah Stephens and eight other garment cutters—nine founders in total.
  • Terence Powderly served as Grand Master Workman from 1879 to 1893 and was elected mayor of Scranton in 1878.
  • Aims included education, mutual aid, cooperation, labor reform, and not only higher wages.
  • Women and Black workers participated; equality was inconsistent and important exclusions remained.
  • 1885: the Knights helped defeat Jay Gould’s railroad system in the Wabash conflict. Membership was about 110,000.
  • By July 1886: about 720,000 members.
  • February 18, 1886: C. A. Hall was fired in Marshall, Texas; the dispute helped trigger the Great Southwest Railroad Strike.
  • By April 1886 the strike was doomed. Moving trains, replacements, injunctions, and armed enforcement helped break it.
  • 1886: Haymarket damaged organized labor’s public standing. The Knights did not organize or control the meeting.
  • By 1893: roughly 75,000 members remained.
  • AFL founded in 1886: narrower craft-union structure, workplace focus, and a more durable institutional model.
The Knights proved that workers could build a national movement. They also exposed how hard it was to keep a huge, diverse movement coordinated against national corporate power.
