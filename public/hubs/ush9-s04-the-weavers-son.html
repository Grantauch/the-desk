Piece 1Yorkshire and LancashireAbout 1806

The best paid workers in town

Around 1806, two kinds of cloth workers in northern England were doing very well.

Croppers in Yorkshire finished wool cloth by hand. They trimmed the fuzzy surface smooth with shears that weighed 40 to 50 pounds. Learning to do it right took years.

Handloom weavers in Lancashire wove cloth at home on looms they owned. They set their own hours. Many kept their loom in an upstairs room with big windows for light.

These were proud, skilled men. Their families lived well on what they made.

2×In 1806 a handloom weaver earned about twice what a cotton factory worker made.

Piece 2Lancashire1800 to 1845

Then the machines arrived

A power loom is a weaving machine driven by water or steam. It does not need a skilled weaver. It needs someone to watch it and fix broken threads.

In Yorkshire, shearing frames did the croppers' job. The cloth came out rougher, but it cost much less to make.

Word spread that an untrained boy on a power loom could weave three and a half pieces of cloth in the time a skilled weaver made one.

Piece 4Nottinghamshire and Yorkshire1811 to 1812

Hammers in the night

It started on March 11, 1811, in Arnold, a village near Nottingham. Stocking knitters broke into workshops and smashed the frames.

Masked men came after dark and were gone before anyone could stop them. Between March 1811 and February 1812 they wrecked about 1,000 machines.

In Yorkshire the croppers carried huge hammers they nicknamed Enoch, after Enoch Taylor, the Marsden blacksmith who built many of the shearing frames. They shouted, "Enoch made them, and Enoch shall break them."

They were not smashing every machine they saw. Around Nottingham, frames owned by employers who paid fair rates were often left alone.

Who was Ned Ludd?

Nobody. The story said an apprentice named Ned Ludd smashed two stocking frames in 1779 after being scolded. Whenever a frame broke, people joked that Ned Ludd did it.

By 1812 the name was a mask. Letters came from General Ludd, King Ludd, and Ned Ludd Clerk. No real leader by that name ever existed, which made the movement very hard to catch.

Trade unions were illegal in Britain from 1799 to 1824. Workers had no legal way to bargain as a group, so they swore secret oaths instead.

Piece 5Parliament, LondonFebruary 1812

Machine breaking becomes a hanging crime

Parliament answered with the Frame Breaking Act in February 1812. A man caught smashing a machine could now be put to death.

The government sent 12,000 soldiers into the textile districts of northern England.

One lord stood up against the law. Lord Byron was 24 years old, a famous poet, and he had seen the hungry towns near his family home in Nottinghamshire. It was his first speech in the House of Lords.

"Can you commit a whole country to their own prisons? Will you erect a gibbet in every field, and hang up men like scarecrows?"Lord Byron, House of Lords, February 27, 1812. A gibbet is a gallows.

The law passed anyway.

    Piece 8Lancashire and Leeds1820 to 1841

    The weaver's son

    Why didn't the weavers just switch jobs? There were not enough jobs to switch to. Some men found work fixing power looms, but far fewer than the number who lost their trade.

    Handloom weavers still outnumbered factory workers in Britain until 1833. Hundreds of thousands stayed at the loom while their pay kept falling.

    Many families were so poor they put their own children to work at the hand loom. A new generation learned a trade that was already dying.

    In 1838, Manchester weavers surveyed 856 of their own families. The average family member lived on about 2 shillings a week.

    The croppers fell even faster. Leeds had 1,733 croppers in 1814. A report from 1841 said the cloth there was now finished by a small number of workers, chiefly boys aged five to eight.

    Former croppers took whatever work they could find. Some became bailiffs, water carriers, or scavengers. Others sold oranges and cakes in the street.

    The children at the machines did not win either. Factory pay was low, the hours were long, and the machines could take a hand.

    Piece 9Britain1815 to 1850

    So who came out ahead?

    Britain made far more cloth than ever before. Somebody got richer. For each person, decide what you think happened, then check the record.

    "I am convinced, that the substitution of machinery for human labour, is often very injurious to the interests of the class of labourers."David Ricardo, economist, 1821. He had believed machines always helped workers. After watching weavers' pay collapse, he added a new chapter to his famous book admitting he was wrong. Portrait by Thomas Phillips, public domain.

    Pay for most British workers did not clearly rise until about 1850. That is a full generation after the hammers.
