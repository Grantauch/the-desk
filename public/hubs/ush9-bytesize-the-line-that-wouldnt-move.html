02 / A DAY THAT NEVER REALLY ENDS

WAIT. WATCH.
DIG. REPEAT.

The trench was a workplace as much as a battlefield. Much of the day meant waiting, digging, carrying, repairing and trying to stay dry. Choose an hour; the work changes.

05:00 / FIRST LIGHT

Stand-to. Every rifle ready.

Before dawn, soldiers took positions expecting an attack at one of the day's most dangerous moments. If nothing came, inspections and breakfast followed. Even an uneventful morning began on edge.

SHIFT LOG / SELECT A TIME

Snipers made exposed daytime work dangerous. In darkness, carrying parties, wiring teams and patrols could move—but were hardly safe.

What cannot wait?

Water collects. Walls collapse. Men need food, clean socks, ammunition and medical care. The war can be quiet without being harmless.

04 / CASE FILE · THE SOMME, 1916

THE COST OF
A FEW MILES.

British and French forces tried to break German defenses along the Somme. Step through four moments. Look for the gap between a new weapon, a local success, and an overall breakthrough.

JULY 1, 1916 / DAY ONE

A week of shelling was not enough.

British leaders expected artillery to destroy German defenses and cut the wire. In many sectors, German defenders survived in dugouts and emerged when the shelling stopped. The infantry went forward into devastating fire. Elsewhere, including parts of the French sector, attacks made important gains. The result was not the planned breakthrough.

THE TECHNOLOGY QUESTION

September 15: a tank enters the story.

British Mark I tanks first fought at Flers–Courcelette. Some helped infantry capture positions. Many broke down or got stuck. A dramatic new machine was not yet an answer to the entire defensive system.

READ THE RESULT CAREFULLY

Did the line actually never move?

It did move, sometimes significantly in individual sectors. The title names the larger failure to achieve the rapid breakthrough leaders expected, not a claim that every trench stayed in place. Geography, weather, command, supplies and adaptations by both sides mattered.

Accuracy notes and limitations

The 475-mile figure is approximate and refers to a complex system, not a single uninterrupted trench. Tactical sketches show relationships without scale. The casualty count is British Army losses on July 1, not the entire battle or all belligerents. The July–November narrative describes a Western Front case study, not every theatre of the First World War. The short assault-planning activity avoids made-up kill rates, random casualty scores or claims that its choices could have guaranteed success.
