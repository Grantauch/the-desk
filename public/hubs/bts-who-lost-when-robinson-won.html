Who Lost When Robinson Won?

Integration was right and overdue. It also emptied one of the largest Black owned businesses in America. Both are true at once.

We are the ship, all else the sea

In 1920 Andrew Rube Foster, a former star pitcher who owned the Chicago American Giants, gathered team owners at the Paseo YMCA in Kansas City and founded the Negro National League. His motto was: We are the ship, all else the sea. He wanted Black baseball owned and run by Black businessmen, so that Black players and fans would not depend on white promoters. The leagues employed players, umpires, bus drivers, ticket sellers and sportswriters in Black neighborhoods across the country.

Gus Greenlee

Built Greenlee Field in Pittsburgh in 1932, one of the few ballparks owned by a Black team owner, and filled his Crawfords with Satchel Paige and Josh Gibson.

Effa Manley

Ran the business side of the Newark Eagles and used the team to raise money for civil rights causes. In 2006 she became the first woman elected to the Baseball Hall of Fame.

The East West Game

The all star game at Chicago's Comiskey Park, played every summer from 1933. In the 1940s it regularly drew crowds above 40,000, the most profitable day of the year for Black baseball.

HOW A GOOD THING CAN STILL COST SOMEBODY

Picture the only pizza place in your neighborhood that hires people from your neighborhood. It's not fancy, but it's yours. Then a giant chain finally agrees to serve your side of town, which it should have done years ago. Everybody can now eat anywhere. That's good. But the best cooks get hired away, the customers drift to the chain, and the local place closes. Nobody who worked there gets paid for what they built.

That's close to what happened to the Negro Leagues. The comparison breaks in one big way, though. The chain didn't just open a store. Major league teams took the local place's best players, often without paying the owners a cent. Saying that out loud doesn't mean integration was wrong. It means the full story has a cost column, and somebody paid it.

      Look at the last two you sorted. Willie Mays and Hank Aaron belong in both columns. They became two of the greatest players ever, and every star who left made the Negro Leagues weaker. The Negro National League played its last season in 1948. The Negro American League hung on with fewer teams and smaller crowds until it was gone by the early 1960s. When Aaron retired in 1976, he was the last Negro League veteran still playing in the majors.
