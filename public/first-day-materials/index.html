1865 → today · Reconstruction to the world you live in

U.S. History 9

Everybody's had a U.S. History class. This is not that class. We open in 1865 with four million people who just got free, a country with absolutely no plan for them, and a Constitution that spent seventy-eight years protecting slavery without ever once printing the word. Week one, you go find it in the actual text — it's in there, hiding in plain sight. Then we follow what happens next: who got power, who took it back, who got killed for trying to vote, and how a railroad map quietly turns into a power map. From there we keep going, all the way to the country you woke up in this morning. Almost all of it is uglier, stranger, and a lot more interesting than the version you got in middle school.

Roswell, then Watergate · one is a real cover-up

Hidden History

Two of the most argued-about stories in American history, and we're going to do the thing almost nobody bothers to do with them — actually check. Not “believe.” Not “debunk.” Check. What does the July 1947 record really say? What did the FBI put in writing that same week? What was on Nixon's tapes, who tried to bury them, and what happened to the guy who refused to fire the prosecutor? One of these two turns out to be a genuine government cover-up with paperwork. The other one turns out to be something more interesting than a cover-up. By the end you'll be able to tell those apart on your own, which is a skill you will use roughly every day of your life, mostly on your phone.

Sports as money, law, and power

Beyond the Scoreboard

A history class that happens to be about sports. In 1876, eight guys shut themselves in a New York hotel and wrote themselves a monopoly that still runs professional sports today. In 1910 the United States government banned a movie — using shipping law — because a Black man won a boxing match. In 1921 a jury said not guilty, and the next morning a man who wasn't a judge banned eight players for life anyway, and nobody could appeal it. Every one of those is real and every one of them is on paper. If you want to understand how power actually works in this country, watching who gets to play and who writes the rules is a shockingly good way in.

The one rule · all three classes
A source proves what it proves. Nothing else.

Every day, something real goes up on the screen. An actual document — the Constitution, an FBI teletype, a bank record, a photo somebody took in 1910. You look at it before I tell you what it means. Then, every time, two questions:

What does this prove?What can it NOT prove?

The second one is the whole class. Anybody can read a document and get excited about it. Knowing exactly where the evidence runs out is the actual skill, and most grown adults on the internet do not have it.

Hidden History

Four words

Every claim you meet lands in one of four buckets. All three classes use these — Hidden History basically lives on them, because the whole course is about claims people argue over. Picking the right bucket is worth more than knowing the date.

ConfirmedThe evidence flat out shows it. You can point at the thing.
SupportedBest explanation we've got, built from a few sources lining up. Strong. Not nailed shut.
UnprovenMight be true. The record just doesn't settle it. Not a polite way of saying false.
MisleadingSomething true in there, but it's getting stretched past where the evidence goes.
U.S. History 9

Claim, detail, limit

Every document gets all three. One thing it proves. One detail worth stealing. One thing it flat-out cannot tell you. Miss that third one and you haven't finished reading — you've just been impressed. Fair warning: Michigan turns up in most of these stories, and not always on the right side of them.

Beyond the Scoreboard

Who could say no?

In 1908, four people had to agree before a heavyweight title fight could happen: the champion, the challenger, a promoter with money, and a state willing to allow it. Any one of them could kill the whole thing by doing nothing. That shape — find every single person who could have said no — works on a league, a law, a company, a courtroom, a school. It's the entire class in one question.

How to actually take notes

There's no packet in this class. No fill-in-the-blanks. You get blank paper, and whatever you build on it is what you carry into the quiz. That freaks people out, so here's exactly what a good page looks like — and a bad one.

Don't do this

Reconstruction was a time when a lot of things happened. Freed people wanted freedom and there were a lot of problems with the government and it was hard for them.

That's a paragraph about how you felt during class. There's nothing in it. No date, no number, no name, nothing you could get asked about. You can't study a vibe.

Do this 
8/31   Freedom on the Ground

4 VERBS:  marry / move / learn / claim
   each one = defense against a specific threat

MARRY -> Bureau records, tens of thousands, 1861-69
   why: inheritance, custody, testimony,
        widow's pension (needed proof)
   marriage under slavery = no legal force

CLAIM -> Field Order 15.  Jan 16, 1865
   Sherman. coastal strip. 40-acre plots
   ~40,000 people farming it by that summer
   ** NOT a national promise - ONE military order **

TAKEN BACK -> fall 1865. Johnson pardons.
   land goes back to the former owners

proves:      freedpeople moved first, on their own
can't prove: that any of it was meant to last
Under the line — repair 
the "mule" = army surplus, handed out informally
never actually written into the order

That's maybe ninety seconds of writing and it beats a full page of sentences. Every single thing on it is something you could get asked about.

Rule

Don't write sentences

Write the thing. Arrows instead of “because” and “this led to.” Nobody is grading your grammar. Ever.

Rule

Numbers and dates, always

If a number is on the screen it goes on your paper. Jan 16, 1865. Forty acres. Forty thousand people. That's what quizzes are made out of.

Rule

Names go down

Sherman. Johnson. Landis. Felt. If I say a name twice, that is not an accident.

Rule

Watch me, not just the slide

If I slow down, repeat myself, or say “this part matters” — that's the tell. Write it.

Rule

Leave the bottom empty

Draw a line across the bottom third of the page before we start. Don't fill it during class. That space has a job.

Rule

Messy is completely fine

Nobody is collecting these. They only have to work for one person and that person is you.

The last five minutes: repair

Step one

Retrieve

Close your notes. Write what you remember from memory. Yes it's uncomfortable. That is the entire point.

Step two

Check

Now look. Find what you missed and what you had flat-out wrong.

Step three

Repair

Fix it under the line. Everything below that line is something you got wrong once — which makes it the most useful part of the whole page.

Being wrong in private, on paper, while it costs you absolutely nothing — that's how you stop being wrong on the quiz. It's the only reason we do this every day.

How ten days work

The month is two ten-day blocks. Both run identically, so the second one has no surprises in it.

Days 1–3Three lectures. New material, blank paper, repair at the end of each one.
Day 4Packet A. On your own. Sorting evidence, tracing causes, writing limits. It is not a vocab worksheet.
Day 5Flex day. Catch up, repair notes, go deeper on something. No new pile of content.
Days 6–8Three more lectures. Same shape.
Day 9Packet B, plus time to fix your notes before the quiz.
Day 10The quiz. Whole block, cumulative, open-note.

Open-note means open your note. Every answer comes from something that was on the screen, in a packet, or that you wrote down. I'm not hiding anything and I'm not trying to trick you. But “open note” does absolutely nothing for a blank page, and somebody finds that out the hard way on Day 10 every single year.

About the room

There's a record player. There are lights. There's a bunch of stuff sitting out where you are instead of locked in a cabinet. That's on purpose — I'd rather this feel like a room than a waiting area.

Here's the deal, though. None of it gets replaced. Not next week, not next month, probably not next year. If something breaks it's just gone, and the room is worse for everybody for the rest of the year — including whoever broke it. So treat it like it's yours, because functionally it is. Put things back where they were. If something's already broken or acting weird, tell me. You are not in trouble for reporting it. You'd be in trouble for me finding it in June.

If you're out

Missing a day

Site first, then somebody's notes, then me. The hub tells you what we did — but the quiz is open-note and cumulative, so the notes are the part that actually matters.

What to bring

Required

A 3-ring binder

Not a folder. Not a stack in the bottom of your bag. Everything in here is loose paper and the quiz is open-note — the binder is the difference between finding something in ten seconds and never finding it at all.

Required

Something to write with

One pen or one pencil. That's the whole ask. Just have it on you.

Optional — but recommended

A second color

Not required, and one color works completely fine. But if you've got a second pen, use it for everything under the repair line. During an open-note quiz you can spot your own fixes from across the page instead of hunting for them. Cheap upgrade.
